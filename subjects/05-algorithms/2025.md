**Derived answer:** B

**Paraphrased problem context:** G is an undirected graph with positive edge weights and T is a minimum spanning tree. Let d1(u,v) be the shortest-path distance in G and d2(u,v) that in T. Which holds for all vertices and all such graphs? A d1=d2; B d1≤d2; C d1≥d2; D d1≠d2.

## Solution

Approach: Compare the sets of paths available in the graph and its spanning-tree subgraph.
The unique u-to-v path in T uses only edges of G. Thus it is one candidate among the paths minimized over when defining d1. Minimizing over a set containing that candidate cannot give a larger value than its length: d1(u,v)≤d2(u,v). Therefore B always holds.
Equality is possible, for example if G itself is a tree. Strict inequality is also possible: take a triangle with all edge weights 1 and a spanning tree consisting of two edges. Between the endpoints of the omitted edge, the graph distance is 1 but the tree distance is 2. These examples reject A and D as universal claims and reject C.
An MST minimizes total chosen-edge weight, not every pairwise path distance. Indeed the inequality holds for any spanning tree; minimum total weight is unnecessary for this particular proof.

**Paraphrased problem context:** T(0)=1 and T(n)=2T(n−1)+n·2^n for n>0. Which asymptotic order is correct? A Θ(n²2^n); B Θ(n2^n); C Θ((log n)²2^n); D Θ(4^n).

## Solution

Approach: Divide out the homogeneous growth factor 2^n so that the remaining recurrence becomes a simple cumulative sum.
Define U(n)=T(n)/2^n. Dividing the recurrence by 2^n gives U(n)=T(n−1)/2^(n−1)+n=U(n−1)+n, with U(0)=1. Repeated substitution yields U(n)=1+1+2+⋯+n=1+n(n+1)/2.
Therefore T(n)=2^n[1+n(n+1)/2]. The bracket is a quadratic polynomial with positive leading coefficient, so it is Θ(n²). Multiplication by 2^n gives Θ(n²2^n), option A.
B overlooks the accumulation of n successive normalized contributions. C grows too slowly by replacing that quadratic sum with logarithms, and D is exponentially larger than the exact solution. A master theorem for T(n/b) is not the natural tool because this recurrence decreases n by one, rather than dividing it.

**Paraphrased problem context:** For i=0,…,n−2, an algorithm scans j=0,…,n−i−2 and swaps A[j],A[j+1] whenever A[j]>A[j+1]. Its input is 30 distinct integers in descending order. How many swaps occur?

## Solution

Approach: An adjacent out-of-order swap removes exactly one inversion, so count the input’s inversions.
An inversion is a pair of positions i<j with A[i]>A[j]. In descending order every pair of distinct positions is an inversion. There are C(30,2)=30×29/2=435 such pairs.
When two adjacent inverted values are swapped, their mutual inversion disappears. Their order relative to every other element contributes the same total as before, because no other element lies between the two positions. Thus each swap reduces the inversion count by exactly one. The shown bubble-sort passes finish in ascending order, with zero inversions, so exactly 435 swaps are required.
A pass-by-pass check gives the same result: the largest value moves right in 29 swaps, the next in 28, and so on down to 1. Summing 29+28+⋯+1 gives 435. Comparisons and swaps are different quantities in general, even though every comparison succeeds for this descending distinct input.

**Official answer:** C

**Derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** An undirected unweighted graph has 100 vertices and maximum finite shortest-path distance 30 between vertices, in the usual connected-graph interpretation. T is any BFS tree. Which holds universally? A height exactly 15; B height exactly 30; C height at least 15; D height at least 30.

## Solution

Approach: A BFS tree preserves distance from its root; relate those root distances to two diameter endpoints.
Let r be the BFS root and let h=max_v d(r,v), the tree height. Choose u,v with d(u,v)=30. A route from u to r and then from r to v has length d(u,r)+d(r,v), so the shortest u-to-v path obeys 30≤d(u,r)+d(r,v)≤h+h=2h. Therefore h≥15, giving C.
Also h≤30 because no pair of vertices is farther apart than the diameter. The actual height depends on the chosen root, so neither exact value in A or B is guaranteed. A path of length 30 rooted at its centre has height 15, while rooting at an endpoint gives height 30. To meet the 100-vertex requirement, attach 69 extra leaves at the central vertex; the diameter remains 30 and the same two heights remain possible.
Thus D’s stronger lower bound 30 is false. BFS preserves root distances, but it does not necessarily choose a central root.

**Paraphrased problem context:** An undirected four-vertex graph has edges AB=7, AD=6, DC=8, BC=x, BD=1, AC=3. The crossing diagonals BD and AC are edges, not an extra intersection vertex. Find the largest integer x for which BC belongs to every minimum spanning tree.

## Solution

Approach: Find a cut on which BC is uniquely cheapest, then test the equality boundary because “every MST” is stronger than “some MST”.
Consider cut {B,D}|{A,C}. Its crossing edges have weights BC=x, DA=6, BA=7 and DC=8. If x<6, BC is the unique lightest edge of this cut. By the cut property it must occur in every MST. In detail, any spanning tree omitting BC has another crossing edge on the path connecting B to C; adding BC and removing that heavier crossing edge lowers its weight, contradicting minimality.
At x=6, the tree {BD,AC,AD} has weight 1+3+6=10 and omits BC. The tree {BD,AC,BC} also has weight 10, and no spanning tree can have smaller weight than the three globally smallest edge weights, which sum to 10. Thus BC is no longer mandatory.
For x>6, BC is uniquely heaviest on cycle B-D-A-C-B, whose other weights are 1,6,3, so the cycle property excludes it from an MST. Therefore the required condition is x<6, and the largest integer is 5. Equality cannot be included just because a tie allows an MST containing BC.

**Official answer:** A

**Paraphrased problem context:** An unordered list contains N distinct integers. In the comparison model, what is the minimum number of element comparisons needed to find any element that is not the largest? Assume N≥2.
Options: A. 1; B. N−1; C. N; D. 2N−1.

## Solution

Compare any two distinct list elements, say a and b. If a<b, return a; otherwise return b. Because the keys are distinct, the returned key is strictly smaller than the other compared key, which also belongs to the list. This is already a certificate that the returned key is not the maximum of the entire list. We do not need to identify the maximum or inspect the other N−2 elements.

One comparison is sufficient and, in the worst-case comparison model, necessary. With zero comparisons there is no ordering evidence about the chosen element; arbitrary distinct input values could make it the largest. N−1 is the familiar bound for finding the actual maximum, which is a stronger problem. The assumption N≥2 is necessary: for N=1 no nonmaximum element exists. Answer: A.

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** B;C;D

**Derived answer:** B;C;D

**Paraphrased problem context:** For BFS and DFS on an undirected simple graph G, choose all true statements.
A. A DFS tree is always a shortest-path tree.
B. Every DFS non-tree edge connects a vertex and an ancestor/descendant, described as a forward/back edge.
C. Endpoints of a BFS non-tree edge have source distances differing by at most one.
D. Either repeated BFS or repeated DFS can find all connected components.

## Solution

A is false. In a triangle with edges s–u,u–v,s–v, DFS may choose tree edges s–u and u–v. Its tree distance from s to v is then 2 even though the graph contains a length-1 path s–v. DFS prioritizes depth, not distance.

B is true for undirected DFS. If an edge from a currently explored vertex reaches an unvisited vertex, DFS follows it before finishing that subtree. Consequently an undirected non-tree edge cannot join two completely separate DFS subtrees; its endpoints have an ancestor-descendant relationship. It is usually called a back edge when oriented toward the ancestor. The question's “forward/back” wording refers to that relationship, not to a separate undirected cross-edge type.

C follows from shortest-path distances. For any edge u–v, a shortest route to u followed by that edge shows d(s,v)≤d(s,u)+1. Reversing the roles gives d(s,u)≤d(s,v)+1. BFS tree levels equal these distances, so their difference is at most one.

D is true: traverse from an unvisited vertex, mark its entire component, and repeat while any vertex remains unvisited. This works with either BFS or DFS, including isolated vertices. Select B,C,D.

**Official answer:** C

**Derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** In an undirected graph with positive edge weights, add the same positive constant α to every edge. How are minimum spanning trees (MSTs) and shortest paths (SPs) affected?
A. Every MST and every SP remain optimal.
B. MSTs may change; every SP remains optimal.
C. Every MST remains optimal; SPs may change.
D. Both MSTs and SPs may change.
Consider components where the corresponding spanning tree/path exists.

## Solution

For a graph with n vertices, every spanning tree uses exactly n−1 edges. Its new weight is w'(T)=w(T)+(n−1)α. Any two trees receive exactly the same increment, so their weight difference and ordering are unchanged. In particular, every old MST is still an MST, and ties between MSTs are preserved.

Paths do not all contain the same number of edges. Consider a triangle with u–w of weight 1, w–v of weight 1, and u–v of weight 3. Initially the two-edge route from u to v costs 2, beating the direct edge's 3. Add α=2 to every edge. The two-edge route now costs 3+3=6, whereas the direct edge costs 5, so the old shortest path is no longer shortest.

Thus constant addition preserves MSTs but need not preserve shortest paths: C. The distinction is a fixed edge count across all spanning trees versus a potentially variable edge count across paths. Multiplying every edge by a positive constant would preserve both, but that is a different operation.

**Paraphrased problem context:** An array A[1…n] has distinct elements and is bitonic: for some 1≤i≤n, A[1…i] is increasing and A[i+1…n] is decreasing. The boundary comparison is not specified. Find the best possible worst-case comparison bound for searching for a supplied key.
Options: A. Θ(n); B. Θ(1); C. Θ((log n)²); D. Θ(log n).

## Solution

Distinctness makes the two monotone parts strict. At the partition boundary, if A[i]<A[i+1], the peak is i+1; otherwise it is i. Thus the array has a single peak, including the possible endpoint peaks of a purely increasing or decreasing array.

Find that peak with binary search on adjacent values. Maintain a candidate interval [lo,hi]. For mid<hi, if A[mid]<A[mid+1], the peak lies strictly to the right of mid; otherwise it lies at mid or to its left. Each comparison discards roughly half the interval, taking O(log n) comparisons. Then binary-search the increasing part in its normal order and the decreasing part with reversed comparison direction. Each search costs O(log n), so their sum, plus peak finding, remains O(log n), not O((log n)²).

For a lower bound, sorted arrays are included among bitonic arrays. In the comparison model, exact membership among n distinct ordered keys has n possible successful positions and requires Ω(log n) comparisons in the worst case. Therefore the attainable bound is Θ(log n): D.

**Paraphrased problem context:** An algorithm runs BFS from any vertex v, picks a farthest vertex u, runs BFS from u, then reports its maximum distance d(u,z). Apply it to the unweighted tree described by these edges (labels added solely to reproduce the source figure): c–l,c–r,c–a,c–b; l–d,l–e,l–g; e–f; g–h,g–i; r–j,r–k; k–m. There are no other edges. Find the reported distance in edges.
Figure orientation: c is the central branching vertex, l its upper-left junction, r its upper-right junction; a,b are the two lower leaves. The left arms end at d,f,h,i and the right arms at j,m.

## Solution

The two-BFS method returns a diameter of an unweighted tree: a farthest vertex from any starting point is a diameter endpoint, and a BFS from that endpoint measures the full diameter. This exact guarantee uses the unique paths of a tree; it should not be assumed for every arbitrary graph.

Compute the diameter directly from the supplied edge list. From central vertex c, the farthest left-side leaves f,h,i are each three edges away: c–l–e–f or c–l–g–h/i. The farthest right-side leaf m is also three edges away: c–r–k–m. Joining a deepest left leaf to m therefore produces a path of length 3+3=6, for example f–e–l–c–r–k–m.

No longer path exists. A path crossing c uses at most two of its branch depths, whose two largest are 3 and 3. A path contained entirely in the left branch has at most 4 edges between f and h/i; a path within the right branch has at most 3 between j and m. The short lower branches of c cannot improve the maximum. Thus the diameter and algorithm's output are 6. The complete 14-vertex, 13-edge tree was reconstructed from the source figure.
