**Derived answer:** B

## Solution

B divides the always-even product n(n−1) by 2 before multiplying by n−2, reducing intermediate magnitude without truncation. The final division by 3 is exact for the integer binomial product. A and D form the larger full product before division, while C may divide n(n−1) by 3 prematurely, truncating when n≡2 mod3. B is less overflow-prone, not guaranteed correct for arbitrarily large unsigned n; even its intermediate can wrap.

**Derived answer:** At most6; official7 is not minimal under algebraic refactoring

**Source access:** Open archive member CS/CS2014.pdf inside the linked official CS.zip. Page numbers refer to the extracted PDF, not the Drive preview.

## Solution

Sparse Horner evaluation gives seven operations, matching the key. But write P(x)=x[(x²+2)²+2]+5. With one temporary t, execute t=x*x; t=t+2; t=t*t; t=t+2; t=t*x; t=t+5. These are three multiplications and three additions, only 6 operations, using the original x plus one temporary. This disproves the official minimum7 unless permitted algebraic transformations are restricted. A global lower bound of 6 is not asserted here; the verified counterexample is sufficient to expose the discrepancy.

**Derived answer:** B

**Source access:** Open archive member CS/CS2014.pdf inside the linked official CS.zip. Page numbers refer to the extracted PDF, not the Drive preview.

## Solution

If x is below the midpoint, j moves to k−1; if above it, i moves to k+1. If equal, both tests fire, setting i=k+1 and j=k−1 and terminating with the matching index k. Thus each unsuccessful iteration shrinks the candidate interval and preserves any possible match, implementing binary search, B. Assume a valid sorted nonempty array and no overflow in i+j.
