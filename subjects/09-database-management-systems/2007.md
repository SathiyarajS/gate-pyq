**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Independently derived answer:** B

**Paraphrased problem context:** Relations are studInfo(studId,name,sex), keyed by studId, and enroll(studId,courseId), keyed by the pair. Every course has at least one male and one female enrollee. What does π_courseId(((π_studId(σ_sex="female"(studInfo))) × π_courseId(enroll)) − enroll) return?
Options:
(A) Courses taken by all female students
(B) Courses taken by a proper subset of female students
(C) Courses taken only by male students
(D) None of these

## Solution

1. Approach. The Cartesian product enumerates required pairs for every female student to take every course. Subtraction identifies missing pairs, and projection asks which courses have at least one such missing pair.

2. Derivation and interpretation. Let F be the set of female student IDs and C the set of course IDs occurring in enroll. The Cartesian product F×C contains every possible female-student/course pair. Subtracting enroll leaves exactly pairs (f,c) for which female f is not enrolled in c. Projecting on courseId therefore returns courses missing at least one female student. Since the premise says every course has at least one female enrollee, these are courses whose female enrollee set is a nonempty proper subset of all female students. This is B, not courses taken by every female student.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** employee(name,sex,supervisorName) has key name. supervisorName names an employee’s supervisor. Interpret the tuple-calculus query {e.name | employee(e) ∧ ∀x[¬employee(x) ∨ x.supervisorName≠e.name ∨ x.sex="male"]}.
Options:
(A) Employees with a male supervisor
(B) Employees with no immediate male subordinates
(C) Employees with no immediate female subordinates
(D) Employees with a female supervisor

## Solution

1. Approach. Rewrite the disjunction as an implication about x: if x is an employee supervised by e, x must be male. Universal conditions are true even when e has no subordinates.

2. Derivation and interpretation. The universal disjunction ¬employee(x) ∨ x.supervisorName≠e.name ∨ x.sex=male is the implication: if x is an employee supervised by e, then x is male. Consequently the result includes employees all of whose immediate subordinates are male, including employees with no subordinates by vacuous truth. Under the male/female domain convention used by the options, this is exactly employees with no immediate female subordinates (C). It says nothing about the sex of e's own supervisor.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** A

**Paraphrased problem context:** employee(empId,name,department,salary) has more than one employee in department 5. We want employees paid more than every department-5 employee. Compare:
Q1: SELECT e.empId FROM employee e WHERE NOT EXISTS (SELECT * FROM employee s WHERE s.department="5" AND s.salary>=e.salary);
Q2: SELECT e.empId FROM employee e WHERE e.salary > ANY (SELECT DISTINCT salary FROM employee s WHERE s.department="5");
Which statement is intended for an arbitrary table under non-NULL salary semantics?
Options:
(A) Q1 is correct
(B) Q2 is correct
(C) Both always produce the same answer
(D) Neither is correct

## Solution

1. Approach. NOT EXISTS rejects the presence of a counterexample to “greater than everyone.” SQL ANY is existential: satisfying one comparison suffices. DISTINCT does not change that quantifier.

2. Derivation and interpretation. For each e, Q1 excludes e whenever any department-5 employee has salary >= e.salary. Thus it keeps exactly salaries strictly greater than the department maximum. Q2 uses > ANY, which means greater than at least one returned salary, not every salary. For department salaries 10 and 20, a salary 15 passes Q2 but not Q1. Hence A under the usual non-NULL salary interpretation. Strict SQL NULL semantics require qualification: NULL comparisons are UNKNOWN, so Q1 can admit NULL salaries or ignore unknown department salaries; no NOT NULL constraint is printed.

**Question:** [Official-source archive](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1popAHO-cX69oQEx_tD0wZXL1tTrBljeO/view), member CS/CS2007.pdf, physical page 15. Consult original wording and choices.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** D

**Paraphrased problem context:** Which normalization statement is intended to be false? Interpret the textbook conventions discussed in the solution.
Options:
(A) Any two-attribute relation is in BCNF
(B) A relation whose every candidate key has one attribute is in 2NF
(C) A prime attribute can be transitively dependent on a key in a 3NF relation
(D) A prime attribute can be transitively dependent on a key in a BCNF relation

## Solution

1. Approach. For a nontrivial dependency X→A, 3NF permits either X to be a superkey or A to be prime. BCNF removes the prime-attribute exception. Definitions of transitive dependence and empty determinants matter here.

2. Derivation and interpretation. Under standard textbook normalization conventions, a two-attribute relation is BCNF: a nontrivial dependency from one attribute to the other makes its determinant a key. Single-attribute candidate keys have no proper nonempty subset, so no partial dependency prevents 2NF. In 3NF a non-key determinant may determine a prime attribute, permitting the transitive dependency in C. BCNF instead requires every nontrivial determinant to be a superkey, so it excludes a transitive dependency through a non-key determinant. D is the intended false statement. Here transitive dependency means the normalization violation through a non-superkey, not merely any composable implication. The claims about two-attribute relations and singleton keys also assume the textbook convention excluding nontrivial empty-determinant dependencies. If such dependencies are allowed, R(A,B) with ∅ -> B and candidate key A violates both BCNF and 2NF: ∅ is not a superkey, and the nonprime B depends on a proper subset of key A. Consequently A and B are also false under that fully general interpretation, so D is the intended answer only with this convention.

**Paraphrased problem context:** The order of a B+ tree leaf is the maximum number of (value,data-record-pointer) pairs it can hold. Block size is 1K bytes, value size 9 bytes, record-pointer size 7 bytes and block-pointer size 6 bytes. What is the leaf order?
Options:
(A) 63
(B) 64
(C) 67
(D) 68

## Solution

1. Approach. Reserve space for the leaf’s next-block pointer before dividing the remaining bytes by entry size. Use 1K=1024 bytes and round the result down, because partial entries cannot be stored.

2. Derivation and interpretation. A leaf stores m pairs of 9-byte values and 7-byte record pointers, plus a 6-byte pointer to the next leaf. Therefore 16m+6 <= 1024, giving m <= floor(1018/16)=63. Answer A. Ignoring the leaf-link pointer would incorrectly give 64.

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** For two transactions, consider schedules S1: r1(X);r1(Y);r2(X);r2(Y);w2(Y);w1(X) and S2: r1(X);r2(X);r2(Y);w2(Y);r1(Y);w1(X). Which are conflict serializable?
Options:
(A) Both
(B) S1 only
(C) S2 only
(D) Neither

## Solution

1. Approach. Create a precedence edge only between different transactions on the same item when at least one access is a write. Read–read pairs do not conflict. A cycle is exactly the obstruction to conflict serializability.

2. Derivation and interpretation. In S1, r1(Y) precedes w2(Y), producing T1 -> T2, while r2(X) precedes w1(X), producing T2 -> T1. The cycle prevents conflict serializability. In S2, w2(Y) precedes r1(Y) and r2(X) precedes w1(X), both giving T2 -> T1; there is no reverse edge. S2 is equivalent to serial order T2,T1. Thus C.
