**Independently derived answer:** B

**Essential problem context (paraphrased):** Why prefer B+-trees to binary trees for disk-resident databases: larger disk capacity, much slower disk access than memory, lower transfer rate alone, or greater disk reliability?

## Solution

Step 1: A B+-tree node can fill a disk page and contain many keys and child pointers. Its high fanout sharply reduces the number of levels compared with a binary tree holding a similar number of keys.

Step 2: A search typically needs one page access per level, so reducing height reduces expensive random disk accesses. The latency difference between disk and memory is the decisive reason, B.

Capacity and reliability do not by themselves explain this branching-factor advantage; transfer rate is distinct from the random-access cost being minimized.

**Essential problem context (paraphrased):** For r(w,x) and s(y,z), when is SELECT DISTINCT w,x FROM r,s guaranteed equal to r? Options: r duplicate-free and s nonempty; both duplicate-free; s duplicate-free and r nonempty; equal tuple counts.

## Solution

Step 1: FROM r,s creates a Cartesian product. If s is nonempty, every row of r appears at least once in that product; projection onto w,x and DISTINCT then returns exactly the distinct rows of r.

Step 2: To preserve SQL bag multiplicities as well, r itself must have no duplicates. No duplicate restriction on s is needed because DISTINCT removes replication.

Hence A. If s is empty, the result is empty even for nonempty r. If r has duplicates, DISTINCT changes it. The other listed conditions do not rule out these failures.

Use standard overflow splitting: a sixth leaf key yields two 3-key leaves, and copy the right leaf minimum into the parent; leaf data keys stay in leaves. Modify insertion: before splitting an overflowing/full target, move one value to its left sibling if that sibling exists and has room; otherwise split. Show the tree after inserting 1 through 9.

## Solution

The final tree under the stated overflow-split convention is:
                 [5]
                /   ╲
      [1 2 3 4] → [5 6 7 8 9]
At the first overflow there is no left sibling, so the first six keys split as usual into [1,2,3] and [4,5,6]. Increasing insertions fill the right leaf through8. To accommodate9, that target leaf would contain [4,5,6,7,8,9], but the left sibling has spare capacity. Move its smallest key4 to the left sibling, and keep [5,6,7,8,9] on the right. Moving the smallest, rather than the largest, preserves the global leaf ordering. Update the parent separator from4 to5 because the right leaf’s minimum changed. No new leaf is needed. Equivalently one can redistribute4 just before inserting9 into the full right leaf. Do not split again merely because the right leaf has five entries: five is its legal maximum; the sixth causes overflow. Separator conventions and split timing must be consistent with part(a).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** Approximately m/2; more precisely the frozen left split size, usually floor((m+1)/2), up to rounding and a boundary leaf.

**Essential problem context (paraphrased):** A B+-tree leaf holds at most m keys. Insert a long increasing sequence using ordinary balanced splits on overflow. What is the approximate average number of keys per leaf?

## Solution

All new larger keys enter the rightmost leaf. When it overflows with m+1 keys, a balanced split leaves about half in the old left leaf and half in a new rightmost leaf. The old left leaf will never receive another key during an increasing insertion sequence. Repeating this process freezes nearly every completed leaf at its half-full split occupancy. Only the final rightmost leaf varies between its initial split size and m, and its contribution to the average vanishes as the number of leaves grows. Therefore the average is approximately m/2, not the roughly69% occupancy sometimes quoted for random insertions. If the convention gives the left leaf floor((m+1)/2) keys, the limiting average is that integer. For m=5 and a3+3 split this is exactly3 in the limit. Rounding and the choice of which side gets an extra key for even m explain why the question asks approximately.

# GATE 2000: Q94 (printed B Q21(c)(ii))

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005203732/GATE2000_compressed-1.pdf#page=10), physical page 10. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** descriptive, Unallocated leaf; group total 5 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** Approximately m keys per leaf, approaching full occupancy.

**Essential problem context (paraphrased):** A B+-tree leaf holds at most m keys. Insert a long increasing sequence, moving a key to the left sibling before splitting whenever that sibling has room. Estimate average leaf occupancy.

## Solution

New keys continue to enter the rightmost leaf. Whenever it overflows, move its smallest key into its left sibling if possible. Repeated overflows progressively fill that left sibling to m entries. Only once that sibling is full must the rightmost leaf split; at that moment the earlier leaves are already full and will receive no more keys. After a split the two rightmost leaves can be partly full, but later insertions fill the older of them by redistribution. Consequently all except at most the final two leaves are full. If there are L leaves, their combined deficit from Lm keys is O(m), independent of L, so average occupancy is m−O(m/L) and tends to m as L grows. This is the improvement over ordinary splitting’s approximately half-full completed leaves for monotone input. Parent separator updates are essential for correctness but do not change the leaf occupancy calculation.

**Independently derived answer:** Self-join by equal account and T2.date<=T1.date, without DISTINCT.

**Essential problem context (paraphrased):** Bank relation transaction(transno,acctno,date,amount) stores positive deposits and negative withdrawals. TP must contain (acctno,T1.date,T2.amount) for every pair of transactions on the same account with T2.date<=T1.date. Define TP in SQL.

## Solution

One SQL definition is:
CREATE VIEW TP (acctno, txn_date, amount) AS
SELECT T1.acctno, T1.date, T2.amount
FROM transaction AS T1
JOIN transaction AS T2
  ON T1.acctno = T2.acctno
 AND T2.date <= T1.date;
Here T1 chooses a balance-observation date and T2 chooses one transaction contributing to that balance. Include equality so the transaction on the observation date contributes; using < would produce the balance before, rather than after, that transaction and omit the first date’s self-pair. Each qualifying transaction pair contributes a row. Do not add DISTINCT: SQL’s bag semantics deliberately preserve separate same-amount transactions even though the projected triples may be identical. For example two deposits of100 on different days generate two copies of (account,later_date,100), both of which must be summed. transaction and date can be reserved identifiers in some SQL dialects; quote the existing identifiers with that dialect’s supported delimiters if needed. Both inspected reproductions show the non-strict inequality, despite OCR text sometimes losing the equality stroke.

# GATE 2000: Q96 (printed B Q22(b))

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005203732/GATE2000_compressed-1.pdf#page=10), physical page 10. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** descriptive, Unallocated leaf; group total 5 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** V sums amounts by account and observation date; final query takes MIN(balance) by account.

**Essential problem context (paraphrased):** Bank relation transaction(transno,acctno,date,amount) stores positive deposits and negative withdrawals. TP must contain (acctno,T1.date,T2.amount) for every pair of transactions on the same account with T2.date<=T1.date. Using only TP, create intermediate view V and query each account’s minimum balance after a transaction, excluding the initial zero. At most one transaction per account per day; each account has at least one transaction.

## Solution

Use two aggregation levels:
CREATE VIEW V (acctno, txn_date, balance) AS
SELECT acctno, txn_date, SUM(amount)
FROM TP
GROUP BY acctno, txn_date;

SELECT acctno, MIN(balance) AS min_balance
FROM V
GROUP BY acctno;
For a fixed account and T1 date, TP has one amount row for every transaction up to and including that date. SUM therefore gives the cumulative balance immediately after the transaction, assuming the opening balance is zero as specified. The one-transaction-per-account-per-day condition ensures each observation date has one T1 row, avoiding repeated copies of the entire prefix caused by multiple T1 transactions on the same day. Taking MIN over these cumulative balances returns the lowest post-transaction balance, including negative balances when appropriate. There is no row for account creation, so the initial zero is not accidentally included; a positive-only account correctly returns its first positive balance rather than zero. An account with amounts100,100,−250 has balances100,200,−50 and minimum−50. DISTINCT in TP would collapse the two100 entries at the later dates and corrupt these sums. Distinct dates, not distinct amounts, define observation groups. Non-null numeric amounts and a comparable chronological date type are implicit in the bank model.
