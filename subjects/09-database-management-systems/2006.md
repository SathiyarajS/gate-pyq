

# GATE 2006: Q20 (printed CS Q20)

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005203119/GATE2006.pdf#page=3), physical page 3. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** B under undo-redo logging

**Paraphrased problem context:** A bank account initially has balance 12000. A transaction transfers 2000 to mortgage M, then another applies 5% interest. Log records are:
1 T1 start
2 T1 B old=1200 new=10000
3 T1 M old=0 new=2000
4 T1 commit
5 T2 start
6 T2 B old=10000 new=10500
7 T2 commit
The old value 1200 in record 2 is printed despite the narrative's 12000. The system crashes just before record 7 is written. Which recovery assertion is true?
Options:
(A) Redo record 6, setting B=10500
(B) Undo record 6 to B=10000, then redo records 2 and 3
(C) Records 2 and 3 need not be redone because T1 committed
(D) Redo and undo can be performed in arbitrary order because they are idempotent

## Solution

1. T1 has a commit record, whereas T2 does not. Undo T2’s balance change to 10000 and ensure T1’s balance/mortgage changes 10000 and 2000 are redone, B.

2. Commit does not necessarily force all modified pages to disk in undo-redo logging; idempotence does not allow arbitrary ordering of conflicting log actions. The reproduced old balance in record 2 is 1200 although the narrative says 12000.

3. That inconsistency does not change the committed new balance or this recovery conclusion, but it is not silently corrected.

4. Teaching check: Recovery distinguishes a transaction whose commit record reached the log from one whose commit record never did. Redo protects committed effects that may not yet be on disk; undo removes effects from incomplete transactions that may already have reached disk. A commit record is therefore not proof that every data page was forced.

# GATE 2006: Q67 (printed CS Q67)

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005203119/GATE2006.pdf#page=10), physical page 10. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** Relation account(customer,balance) has customer as primary key and no nulls. Rank by decreasing balance, starting at 1, tying equal balances and skipping ranks (two tied maxima both rank 1, next rank 3).
Q1: SELECT A.customer,COUNT(B.customer) FROM account A,account B WHERE A.balance<=B.balance GROUP BY A.customer;
Q2: SELECT A.customer,1+COUNT(B.customer) FROM account A,account B WHERE A.balance<B.balance GROUP BY A.customer;
Statements: 1 Q1 and Q2 agree for some but not all databases; 2 both implement the rank specification; 3 Q1 is correct but Q2 is not; 4 neither is correct; 5 pure relational ranking is faster than scanning in decreasing balance order and assigning ranks through ODBC. Which pair is correct?
Options:
(A) 2 and 5
(B) 1 and 3
(C) 1 and 4
(D) 3 and 5

## Solution

1. Query1 counts B rows with A.balance≤B.balance, so a customer tied with t customers at its balance receives the count of higher-balance customers plus t, instead of plus 1. For two tied maximum balances it gives 2, not 1.

2. Query2 uses A.balance<B.balance and adds 1 to the count, but its inner join produces no group for maximum-balance customers; those customers disappear. Thus neither query is correct (statement 4).

3. They agree on the empty database but differ on every nonempty database because Query1 includes the maximum group and Query2 does not, establishing statement 1 under the allowed empty-database case. Hence statements 1 and 4, option C.

4. Statement 5 is not a general performance guarantee.

5. Teaching check: The intended rank is 1 plus the number of strictly higher balances. Q1 instead counts all tied customers along with higher ones. Q2 uses the correct comparison but an inner join removes maxima, so adding one to its count does not restore the missing rows.

# GATE 2006: Q68 (printed CS Q68)

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005203119/GATE2006.pdf#page=10), physical page 10. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** B

**Paraphrased problem context:** Schemas enrolled(student,course), key (student,course), and paid(student,amount), key student; no nulls, foreign keys or additional constraints.
Q1: SELECT student FROM enrolled WHERE student IN (SELECT student FROM paid);
Q2: SELECT student FROM paid WHERE student IN (SELECT student FROM enrolled);
Q3: SELECT E.student FROM enrolled E,paid P WHERE E.student=P.student;
Q4: SELECT student FROM paid WHERE EXISTS (SELECT * FROM enrolled WHERE enrolled.student=paid.student);
Which statement holds under SQL result-bag semantics (no DISTINCT)?
Options:
(A) All four return identical rows for every database
(B) Q2 and Q4 always agree, but Q1 and Q2 can differ
(C) Q3 can return strictly fewer rows than Q2
(D) Q4 can encounter a runtime integrity violation

## Solution

1. Query2 and Query4 each scan paid, whose student key is unique, and keep exactly students with an enrolled match. They therefore return identical bags with one row per matching student.

2. Query1 scans enrolled and returns one student occurrence for each matching enrollment; Query3 has the same multiplicities because paid has at most one matching row. A paid student enrolled in two courses appears twice in Query1/3 but once in Query2/4.

3. Thus B. SELECT alone does not eliminate duplicate rows; the phrase row sets must be interpreted as SQL result rows/bags.

4. Teaching check: A membership predicate filters each outer row but does not collapse duplicates already present in the outer projection. The paid key guarantees one outer row per student for Q2/Q4, whereas enrolled can contain several courses per student for Q1/Q3. Relational set intuition must not erase SQL multiplicities.

# GATE 2006: Q69 (printed CS Q69)

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005203119/GATE2006.pdf#page=10), physical page 10. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** B

**Paraphrased problem context:** Schemas enrolled(student,course), key (student,course), and paid(student,amount), key student, have no nulls, foreign keys or additional constraints. Amounts 6000,7000,8000,9000,10000 are each paid by 20% of students.
Both plans list courses of students paying more than x. Plan 1 sequentially scans paid, selects amount>x, probes enrolled's student index for an indexed nested-loop join, then projects course. Plan 2 scans all paid and performs the same indexed join first, then selects amount>x and projects course. Projection does not request DISTINCT. A disk seek costs 4 ms, transfer rate is 300 MB/s, and checking amount>x costs 10 μs per tuple. Which statement holds?
Options:
(A) The plans do not return identical rows for every database
(B) Plan 1 may list a course more than once
(C) At x=5000 Plan 1 is faster for every database
(D) At x=9000 Plan 1 is slower for every database

## Solution

1. Both plans select the same matching paid/enrolled tuples: pushing a predicate on paid.amount below the join preserves the result. Projection without DISTINCT can output the same course for two different qualifying students, so B is true under SQL bag semantics.

2. The enrolled key is (student,course), not course. C is not universal: at x=5000 all paid rows pass, and with no enrollments Plan 2 has no joined rows to test while Plan 1 still tests paid rows.

3. D is not universal either: at x=9000 Plan 1 can avoid 80% of index probes, making it faster on ordinary populated instances. Seek and transfer constants do not turn either universal performance claim into a theorem.

4. The plans/options continue on page 11.

5. Teaching check: Pushing a filter before a join can reduce work, but a universal speed claim needs more than a selectivity estimate. Course is not a key of the join output, so two qualifying students in the same course produce repeated course values after projection without DISTINCT.

# GATE 2006: Q70 (printed CS Q70)

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005203119/GATE2006.pdf#page=11), physical page 11. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** C and D as reproduced

**Paraphrased problem context:** Given dependencies AB→CD, AF→D, DE→F, C→G, F→E, G→A, which claimed attribute closure is false? Both reproductions contain the following options, including F in D.
Options:
(A) (CF)⁺={A,C,D,E,F,G}
(B) (BG)⁺={A,B,C,D,G}
(C) (AF)⁺={A,C,D,E,F,G}
(D) (AB)⁺={A,B,C,D,F,G}

## Solution

1. Apply AB→CD, AF→D, DE→F, C→G, F→E, G→A until closure stops. (CF)+={A,C,D,E,F,G}: C gives G then A; F gives E; AF gives D.

2. (BG)+={A,B,C,D,G}: G gives A then AB gives C,D; neither E nor F is derivable. (AF)+={A,D,E,F}: AF gives D and F gives E, but nothing yields C or G.

3. (AB)+={A,B,C,D,G}: AB gives C,D, C gives G; no E or F follows. Thus C is false and D is also false because its printed right side includes F.

4. A and B are true. There is no unique single false option in these reproductions.

5. Teaching check: Begin each closure with the attributes on the left, then repeatedly add a dependency’s right side only when its entire left side is present. In particular DE→F cannot fire with D alone. This is why AB cannot acquire E or F and why AF cannot acquire C or G.
