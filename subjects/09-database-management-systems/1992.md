**Independently derived answer:** Store shared parent information once above its children; systems supporting logical/virtual child links can reference a shared record rather than duplicate it.

**Essential problem context (paraphrased):** Give a one-line answer: how is redundancy reduced in the hierarchical database model?

## Solution

One-line answer: Store shared parent information once above its children; systems supporting logical/virtual child links can reference a shared record rather than duplicate it.

Why this works: A parent such as a department need not have its name and address copied into every employee child. A pure tree, however, gives each child one parent and does not automatically remove duplication for many-to-many relationships. Logical relationships or virtual/pointer records are implementation extensions, not a universal property of every hierarchical model.

Redundancy means repeatedly storing the same fact, not merely having repeated key values. Keys or links may legitimately recur to express relationships. These are design mechanisms rather than a claim that the model makes every possible database free of redundancy.

## Solution

One-line answer: Store an entity record once and represent its multiple owner/member relationships by links instead of repeating its data.

Why this works: A network model allows a record to participate in multiple set relationships. A course shared by several student relationships can be stored as one course record with relationship links or connector records, rather than duplicating the course attributes for each student. The relationships still occupy space; the reduction is in repeated entity facts.

Redundancy means repeatedly storing the same fact, not merely having repeated key values. Keys or links may legitimately recur to express relationships. These are design mechanisms rather than a claim that the model makes every possible database free of redundancy.

**Independently derived answer:** Normalize relations using their dependencies, decomposing repeated facts into linked relations with keys.

**Essential problem context (paraphrased):** Give a one-line answer: how is redundancy reduced in the relational database model?

## Solution

One-line answer: Normalize relations using their dependencies, decomposing repeated facts into linked relations with keys.

Why this works: For example, a relation Employee(emp,dept,deptName) repeats deptName for every employee in a department when dept→deptName. Decompose it into Employee(emp,dept) and Department(dept,deptName), with dept as the latter key. The department fact is then stored once. Decomposition should preserve information through a lossless join, and dependency preservation is desirable. Being relational alone does not guarantee a normalized schema.

Redundancy means repeatedly storing the same fact, not merely having repeated key values. Keys or links may legitimately recur to express relationships. These are design mechanisms rather than a claim that the model makes every possible database free of redundancy.

# GATE 1992: Q62 (printed B Q13(b))

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005204205/GATE1992.pdf#page=9), physical page 9. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** descriptive, Unallocated leaf; group total None mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** π_HOTEL(SERVES ⋈ σ_CUSTOMER='Rama'(LIKES)).

**Essential problem context (paraphrased):** Relations are FREQUENTS(CUSTOMER,HOTEL), SERVES(HOTEL,SNACKS), LIKES(CUSTOMER,SNACKS). They record visits, available snacks, and preferences respectively. Express in relational algebra the query: list hotels that serve a snack customer Rama likes.

## Solution

First select Rama's preferences: R = σ_CUSTOMER='Rama'(LIKES). Next match those snack names with served snacks by a natural join on their common attribute SNACKS. Finally discard the customer and snack columns, retaining only hotels:

π_HOTEL(SERVES ⋈ σ_CUSTOMER='Rama'(LIKES)).

An equivalent expression first projects preferences to snack names:
π_HOTEL(SERVES ⋈ π_SNACKS(σ_CUSTOMER='Rama'(LIKES))).

For any hotel h in this result, the join supplies some snack s with SERVES(h,s) and LIKES('Rama',s), exactly the requested existential condition. Conversely, every such pair joins and projects to h, so no qualifying hotel is omitted. Under ordinary set relational algebra projection removes duplicates when a hotel serves several liked snacks.

FREQUENTS is deliberately unused: the query does not require Rama to visit the hotel. Joining it and filtering Rama would incorrectly exclude unvisited hotels. Nor is division appropriate: division would express serving every liked snack, whereas the wording asks for at least one. If Rama likes no snacks, the result is empty.
