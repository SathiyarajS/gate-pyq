**Paraphrased problem context:** Let L1,L2 be arbitrary context-free languages and R an arbitrary regular language. Which claims always hold? I:L1∪L2 is context-free. II:complement(L1) is context-free. III:L1−R is context-free. IV:L1∩L2 is context-free. A:I,II,IV. B:I,III. C:II,IV. D:I only.

## Solution

1. I holds: take disjoint copies of the two grammars and add a new start production S→S1|S2. The resulting CFG generates their union.

2. III also holds because L1−R=L1∩complement(R). Regular languages are closed under complement, and a PDA for L1 can be combined with a finite automaton for complement(R). Thus the intersection remains context-free.

3. IV fails in general: {a^n b^n c^m} and {a^m b^n c^n} are CFLs, but their intersection {a^n b^n c^n} is not. II cannot hold for all CFLs either, since closure under both complement and union would imply intersection closure by De Morgan's law. Only I and III are guaranteed, B.

## Solution

3. Conversely, if m>n≥0, choose k=m-n≥1 in X and n matching pairs in Y to derive the desired string. The characterization is exact, C. For example a is generated, ruling out options requiring n>0; equal counts are impossible because X supplies extra a symbols.

## Solution

1. The first two symbols may be arbitrary, the third must be a, and at least three further symbols must follow. Thus accepted strings have length at least 6 and third symbol a.

2. Construct three states for having read 0,1,2 prefix symbols. From the third of these, a enters a suffix-count state and b enters a rejecting dead state. 3. To prove minimality, the seven nondead states have distinct shortest accepting continuation lengths 6,5,4,3,2,1,0. For two states, a shortest accepting suffix from the one with smaller required length is too short from the other, distinguishing them. The dead state has no accepting continuation at all. All eight are necessary.

IV is true: a deterministic PDA pushes for a symbols, switches on the first b and pops for each b, accepting exactly matching positive counts. Therefore III and IV, D.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Which problems are undecidable? I:given regular expression R and string w, test w∈L(R). II:given CFG G, test L(G)=∅. III:given CFG G over Σ, test L(G)=Σ*. IV:given Turing machine M and string w, test w∈L(M). A:I,IV. B:II,III. C:II,III,IV. D:III,IV.

## Solution

1. I is decidable: convert the regular expression to a finite automaton and simulate it on the finite input string. The simulation terminates after processing the input.

2. II is decidable: repeatedly mark CFG variables that can derive terminal strings, beginning with terminal-only or ε productions. Because there are finitely many variables the marking reaches a fixed point. The language is nonempty exactly when the start variable is marked.

3. III is the general CFG universality problem, which is undecidable. IV is Turing-machine acceptance, also undecidable: simulation may run forever on a rejected/nonaccepted input, so it is only a recognizer, not a decider. Thus the undecidable pair is III and IV, D. Do not confuse decidable CFG membership or emptiness with universality.

## Solution

1. If f is computable, first reject strings not of form x#y with x∈A*,y∈B*. Otherwise compute f(x), which halts by totality, and compare it with y. This is a decider for Lf, so the graph is recursive and hence RE.

3. Because f is total, one candidate y=f(x) exists and will eventually be accepted; function uniqueness ensures it is the right output. This procedure halts for every x and computes f. Thus an RE graph already suffices, and a recursive graph certainly suffices. Both A and B are mathematically true; the protected official single-choice answer A is retained, not treated as eliminating B.
