

## Q25 — Theory of Computation: grammar ambiguity and derivations

Paper page 20. **Answer: A, B, C.** Official key agrees.

Work with the grammar S → aSbS | bS | ε. Here ε denotes the empty string.

1. The string abb admits these two different leftmost derivations:
   - S ⇒ aSbS ⇒ abS ⇒ abbS ⇒ abb.
   - S ⇒ aSbS ⇒ abSbS ⇒ abbS ⇒ abb.
   In the first derivation, the extra b comes from the final S of the root production. In the second, it comes from the first S. Thus they represent different parse trees, not just different orders of expanding one tree. Both ambiguity (A) and the existence of two distinct derivations (B) follow.
2. For abab, the root must use aSbS because the word starts with a. Its explicit b must be the b in position 2 or position 4. Choosing position 4 would require the first S to generate ba. That is impossible: every nonempty word generated by S ends in b. To prove this invariant, bS ends in b if its child is empty or ends in b if its child does; aSbS has the same property through its explicit b and final child.
3. Consequently the explicit root b is in position 2, its first child is ε, and its final child generates ab. The latter has only the construction aSbS with both children empty. Therefore abab has exactly one parse tree and one rightmost derivation, establishing C. One rightmost derivation is S ⇒ aSbS ⇒ aSbaSbS ⇒ aSbaSb ⇒ aSbab ⇒ abab.
4. This is a context-free grammar. Membership in a context-free language is decidable, for example by converting to a suitable normal form and applying a finite parsing algorithm. Therefore the undecidability claim D is false.

**Common error:** Two unrestricted derivations can differ solely in expansion order. Ambiguity requires different parse trees, equivalently different leftmost derivations or different rightmost derivations; the witness above meets that stronger test.

## Q26 — Theory of Computation: NFA-to-DFA state bound

Paper page 20. **Answer: B, D.** Official key agrees.

1. Subset construction represents each DFA state by a subset of the six NFA states. There are at most 2^6 = 64 such subsets, including the empty subset. Epsilon closures, if applicable, do not increase this bound.
2. Removing unreachable states and minimizing can only reduce that number. A minimal equivalent DFA therefore cannot require 65 or 128 states, establishing B and D.
3. One state is possible: a six-state NFA can accept every string. For an example with all six states reachable, use a cycle of six accepting states over a one-letter alphabet. Its language is universal and its minimal DFA has one accepting state.
4. To check that 32 is genuinely possible, use the binary language whose fifth symbol from the end is 1. A six-state NFA has one start state that loops on 0 and 1, can nondeterministically guess a 1 as that fifth-from-last symbol, and then traverses four more symbol transitions to an accepting state. A 32-state DFA stores the last five symbols, initially padded with zeros. Any distinct five-bit histories differ in some position j; appending j−1 zeros makes that position the fifth symbol from the end. The two resulting strings have different acceptance outcomes. Thus all 32 histories are distinguishable and the minimal DFA has exactly 32 states.

**Common error:** An upper bound alone rules out the oversized choices; an explicit witness is useful to verify that the smaller offered values really are possible.

# GATE 2026 CS1: Q51

**Question:** [Official paper page 39](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2026/QPs/CS1.pdf#page=39); refer to the original wording and options.

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 2 marks.

**Official answer:** C

## Solution

Every regular language is context-free, so C follows immediately from the given regularity of L2. The intersection condition does not impose comparable restrictions on the part of L1 outside L2. Choose L2 to be empty and L1={a^n b^n c^n : n>=0}. Then L2 and L1 intersection L2 are both regular, while L1 is not context-free and hence not regular. Also L1 union L2 equals L1 and is not regular. This single counterexample refutes A, B and D, leaving only C.

## Q52 — Theory of Computation: counting invariants in a grammar

Paper page 40. MSQ, 2 marks. **Answer: B, D.**

Write a(w) and b(w) for the numbers of the two terminals in a generated word w.

1. Every expansion of B produces a^k b^k for some k≥1. Hence its two counts are equal.
2. For A's terminating alternative, a B expansion with k of each terminal gives counts a=k+3 and b=k+2. Thus b≤a≤2b.
3. Suppose a word derived from the recursive A child has counts x,y satisfying y≤x≤2y. The surrounding recursive production contributes two a terminals and one b terminal, plus two B words. If those B words together contribute K of each terminal, the new counts are x+K+2 and y+K+1. Their difference is (x−y)+1≥0. Also twice the new b count minus the new a count is (2y−x)+K≥0. Thus both inequalities are invariant under recursion.
4. The start production combines two A words, one B word and an outer string with three a terminals and three b terminals. Adding the counts preserves b≤a≤2b. Consequently every complete word satisfies the two bounds in B and D. The existential violations in A and C are impossible.

**Common error:** Counting only the explicitly written terminals of a production omits arbitrarily long balanced B expansions. The invariant accounts for them without enumerating strings.
