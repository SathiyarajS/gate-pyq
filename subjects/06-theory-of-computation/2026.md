**Original question:** [Complete wording, options and figures](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2026/QPs/CS1.pdf#page=20).

**Paraphrased problem context:** For the grammar S→aSbS | bS | ε, decide: A the grammar is ambiguous; B abb has two distinct derivations; C abab has only one rightmost derivation; D the generated language is undecidable.

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 1.

**Official answer:** A;B;C

**Derived answer:** A;B;C

## Solution

Givens and goal
For the grammar S→aSbS | bS | ε, decide: A the grammar is ambiguous; B abb has two distinct derivations; C abab has only one rightmost derivation; D the generated language is undecidable.

Approach
Exhibit different parse structures for the ambiguity claim, prove uniqueness for the specific other word, and use the decidability of context-free membership.

Step-by-step solution
Work with the grammar S → aSbS | bS | ε. Here ε denotes the empty string.

1. The string abb admits these two different leftmost derivations:
   - S ⇒ aSbS ⇒ abS ⇒ abbS ⇒ abb.
   - S ⇒ aSbS ⇒ abSbS ⇒ abbS ⇒ abb.
   In the first derivation, the extra b comes from the final S of the root production. In the second, it comes from the first S. Thus they represent different parse trees, not just different orders of expanding one tree. Both ambiguity (A) and the existence of two distinct derivations (B) follow.
2. For abab, the root must use aSbS because the word starts with a. Its explicit b must be the b in position 2 or position 4. Choosing position 4 would require the first S to generate ba. That is impossible: every nonempty word generated by S ends in b. To prove this invariant, bS ends in b if its child is empty or ends in b if its child does; aSbS has the same property through its explicit b and final child.
3. Consequently the explicit root b is in position 2, its first child is ε, and its final child generates ab. The latter has only the construction aSbS with both children empty. Therefore abab has exactly one parse tree and one rightmost derivation, establishing C. One rightmost derivation is S ⇒ aSbS ⇒ aSbaSbS ⇒ aSbaSb ⇒ aSbab ⇒ abab.
4. This is a context-free grammar. Membership in a context-free language is decidable, for example by converting to a suitable normal form and applying a finite parsing algorithm. Therefore the undecidability claim D is false.

**Common error:** Two unrestricted derivations can differ solely in expansion order. Ambiguity requires different parse trees, equivalently different leftmost derivations or different rightmost derivations; the witness above meets that stronger test.

Parse-tree distinction for abb can be made explicit by writing the root’s first and last S expansions as a pair. One tree has root a S b S with child words (ε,b); the other has child words (b,ε). Both concatenate to abb, but they attach the additional b to different children. This makes the ambiguity evidence structural, not just a consequence of expanding independent children in different orders.

**Original question:** [Complete wording, options and figures](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2026/QPs/CS1.pdf#page=20).

**Paraphrased problem context:** An NFA has six states over a finite alphabet. Which values cannot be the number of states of its minimal equivalent DFA: A 32, B 65, C 1, D 128?

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 1.

**Official answer:** B;D

**Derived answer:** B;D

## Solution

Givens and goal
An NFA has six states over a finite alphabet. Which values cannot be the number of states of its minimal equivalent DFA: A 32, B 65, C 1, D 128?

Approach
Use subset construction for an upper bound and explicit languages to verify the offered feasible values.

Step-by-step solution
1. Subset construction represents each DFA state by a subset of the six NFA states. There are at most 2^6 = 64 such subsets, including the empty subset. Epsilon closures, if applicable, do not increase this bound.
2. Removing unreachable states and minimizing can only reduce that number. A minimal equivalent DFA therefore cannot require 65 or 128 states, establishing B and D.
3. One state is possible: a six-state NFA can accept every string. For an example with all six states reachable, use a cycle of six accepting states over a one-letter alphabet. Its language is universal and its minimal DFA has one accepting state.
4. To check that 32 is genuinely possible, use the binary language whose fifth symbol from the end is 1. A six-state NFA has one start state that loops on 0 and 1, can nondeterministically guess a 1 as that fifth-from-last symbol, and then traverses four more symbol transitions to an accepting state. A 32-state DFA stores the last five symbols, initially padded with zeros. Any distinct five-bit histories differ in some position j; appending j−1 zeros makes that position the fifth symbol from the end. The two resulting strings have different acceptance outcomes. Thus all 32 histories are distinguishable and the minimal DFA has exactly 32 states.

**Common error:** An upper bound alone rules out the oversized choices; an explicit witness is useful to verify that the smaller offered values really are possible.

Subset-construction interpretation: after reading a prefix, an NFA may be in several states at once. A deterministic simulator records exactly that reachable subset. With six possible states, each independently present or absent, there are 64 possible records; different prefixes need not reach all of them. This is why 64 is an upper bound, not a claim that every six-state NFA requires 64 DFA states.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Languages L₁ and L₂ share a finite alphabet. Both L₂ and L₁∩L₂ are regular. Which conclusions always hold: A L₁ regular; B L₁∪L₂ regular; C L₂ context-free; D L₁ context-free?

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 2.

**Official answer:** C

**Derived answer:** C

## Solution

Givens and goal
Languages L₁ and L₂ share a finite alphabet. Both L₂ and L₁∩L₂ are regular. Which conclusions always hold: A L₁ regular; B L₁∪L₂ regular; C L₂ context-free; D L₁ context-free?

Approach
Use the inclusion of regular languages in context-free languages, then choose a regular L₂ that hides all complexity in L₁.

Step-by-step solution
1. Every regular language is context-free: for example, a finite automaton can be converted into a right-linear grammar, which is a context-free grammar. Since L₂ is given regular, C must hold.
2. To test the other claims, let L₂=∅ and L₁={a^n b^n c^n:n≥0}. The alphabet {a,b,c} is finite as required.
3. The empty language is regular, and L₁∩L₂=∅ is also regular. Thus this choice satisfies both hypotheses no matter how complicated L₁ is.
4. The equal-three-block language L₁ is not context-free, hence is not regular either. The usual context-free pumping argument takes a^p b^p c^p: a pumpable region of length at most p cannot span all three blocks, so pumping changes at most two counts or destroys the block order while at least one count remains p.
5. Therefore A and D fail. Also L₁∪L₂=L₁, so B fails for the same example.
6. Only C is guaranteed. Regularity of an intersection controls the part of L₁ inside L₂, not the potentially non-context-free part outside L₂.

**Original question:** [Complete wording, options and figures](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2026/QPs/CS1.pdf#page=40).

**Paraphrased problem context:** The grammar is S→abaABA bba (spaces only separate symbols), A→aaBBA b | bB abaa, and B→aBb | ab. For a generated string s let n₁ count a’s and n₂ count b’s. Assess A: some n₁<n₂; B: always n₁≥n₂; C: some n₁>2n₂; D: always n₁≤2n₂.

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 2.

**Official answer:** B;D

**Derived answer:** B;D

## Solution

Givens and goal
The grammar is S→abaABA bba (spaces only separate symbols), A→aaBBA b | bB abaa, and B→aBb | ab. For a generated string s let n₁ count a’s and n₂ count b’s. Assess A: some n₁<n₂; B: always n₁≥n₂; C: some n₁>2n₂; D: always n₁≤2n₂.

Approach
Track the pair of terminal counts through productions and prove invariant inequalities by induction rather than enumerate all words.

Step-by-step solution
Write a(w) and b(w) for the numbers of the two terminals in a generated word w.

1. Every expansion of B produces a^k b^k for some k≥1. Hence its two counts are equal.
2. For A's terminating alternative, a B expansion with k of each terminal gives counts a=k+3 and b=k+2. Thus b≤a≤2b.
3. Suppose a word derived from the recursive A child has counts x,y satisfying y≤x≤2y. The surrounding recursive production contributes two a terminals and one b terminal, plus two B words. If those B words together contribute K of each terminal, the new counts are x+K+2 and y+K+1. Their difference is (x−y)+1≥0. Also twice the new b count minus the new a count is (2y−x)+K≥0. Thus both inequalities are invariant under recursion.
4. The start production combines two A words, one B word and an outer string with three a terminals and three b terminals. Adding the counts preserves b≤a≤2b. Consequently every complete word satisfies the two bounds in B and D. The existential violations in A and C are impossible.

**Common error:** Counting only the explicitly written terminals of a production omits arbitrarily long balanced B expansions. The invariant accounts for them without enumerating strings.

Terminal-count bookkeeping:

    Production component             Added a's   Added b's
    A→bB abaa, excluding B                 3           2
    A→aaBBA b, excluding B,B,A             2           1
    S→aba A B A bba, excluding A,B,A       3           3

For example, choosing B→ab and the terminating A alternative gives each A counts (4,3). The shortest choices in S therefore yield totals (3,3)+(4,3)+(1,1)+(4,3)=(12,10), consistent with both bounds. This example illustrates the invariant but the induction, not the example alone, proves it for arbitrarily deep recursion.

**Paraphrased problem context:** For a Turing machine M and L⊆{0,1}*, which is equivalent to M deciding L? A M halts everywhere; B M accepts all members of L; C M rejects all nonmembers; D M accepts every member and rejects every nonmember.

## Solution

Givens and goal
For a Turing machine M and L⊆{0,1}*, which is equivalent to M deciding L? A M halts everywhere; B M accepts all members of L; C M rejects all nonmembers; D M accepts every member and rejects every nonmember.

Approach
A decider must be both correct and terminating on every input.

Step-by-step solution
1. A decision procedure answers the membership question for every finite binary input. When the input belongs to L the answer must be acceptance, and otherwise it must be rejection.
2. Both accepting and rejecting are halting outcomes. Thus D guarantees termination on every input as well as the correct result, and exactly captures the definition.
3. A alone permits an always-accept machine even when L is a proper subset of {0,1}*. B permits accepting nonmembers too, or failing to halt on them. C permits wrongly rejecting members or looping on them.
4. Therefore D is necessary and sufficient. A recognizer need only accept precisely the members, and may loop on nonmembers; a decider has the additional total-halting requirement.

## Solution

Givens and goal
Which grammars are ambiguous? A S→aSb|ε; B E→E+E|E*E|id; C S→aS|Sa|ε; D S→aS|ε.

Approach
Exhibit two parse trees for ambiguous choices and explain uniqueness for the others.

Step-by-step solution
1. B generates id+id*id with root production E→E+E, whose right child expands by multiplication. It also generates the same string with root E→E*E, whose left child expands by addition. These are different tree structures, corresponding to id+(id*id) and (id+id)*id.
2. C already has two trees for the one-letter string a: use S→aS→aε, or S→Sa→εa. The root productions differ, so this is genuine ambiguity, not merely a different order of expanding independent nodes.
3. For A, every nonempty string is aⁿbⁿ. The first rule must be aSb and must be applied exactly n times, followed by ε. No alternative tree is possible for that n.
4. For D, a string aⁿ requires exactly n applications of aS followed by ε. Again the chain is unique.
5. Therefore B,C are ambiguous. The existence of an unambiguous grammar for the same language would not remove ambiguity from these particular grammars.

D1: s−0→u, s−1→v, u−0→w, u−1→z, w−0→s, w−1→s, z−0→s, v−0→t, t−1→s. D2: s−0→u, s−1→v, u−1→w, v−0→t, v−1→t and v−1→w, w−1→s, t−0→s. ## Solution

Givens and goal
Transcribe the source automata as follows; s is the only start/accept state and omitted transitions reject. D1: s−0→u, s−1→v, u−0→w, u−1→z, w−0→s, w−1→s, z−0→s, v−0→t, t−1→s. D2: s−0→u, s−1→v, u−1→w, v−0→t, v−1→t and v−1→w, w−1→s, t−0→s. Select: A equal languages; B L(D1) proper subset of L(D2); C intersection {ε}; D (L(D1)∪L(D2))* is all binary strings of length divisible by 3.

Approach
Enumerate the three-edge returns to the accepting state and exploit unique block boundaries.

Step-by-step solution
1. In D1 the upper paths give 000 and 001 through u,w, and 010 through u,z. The lower path through v,t gives 101. Thus L(D1)={000,001,010,101}*, since every accepting return to s has exactly three symbols.
2. In D2 the path through u,w gives 011. Paths through v,t give 100 and 110, and the path through v,w gives 111. Hence L(D2)={011,100,110,111}*. The two transitions from v on 1 are nondeterministic alternatives, both included.
3. These two block sets are disjoint. A nonempty string of length divisible by three has a unique partition into successive three-bit blocks starting at position 1. Its first block cannot simultaneously belong to both sets, so the languages have no common nonempty word.
4. Both automata accept ε because their start state is accepting. Thus the intersection is exactly {ε}, proving C. The word 000 is accepted only by D1, refuting both equality and the claimed subset in A and B.
5. Together the two block sets contain all eight three-bit words. Kleene star of the union permits concatenations mixing blocks from either automaton, yielding every binary word of length 3k, including k=0. Conversely each concatenated component has length divisible by 3. This proves D.
6. Select C,D. The outer star in D is essential: the plain union need not accept a word whose successive blocks alternate between the two sets. Transitions were verified on the source image.

**Question:** [Official master paper page 34](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2026/QPs/CS2.pdf#page=34); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** A;C;D

**Derived answer:** A;C;D

**Paraphrased problem context:** Consider strings a^i b^j c^k d^ℓ with i,j,k,ℓ≥0. Which restrictions yield context-free languages? A i+k=j+ℓ; B i=k and j=ℓ; C i=ℓ and j=k; D i+j=k+ℓ.

## Solution

Givens and goal
Consider strings a^i b^j c^k d^ℓ with i,j,k,ℓ≥0. Which restrictions yield context-free languages? A i+k=j+ℓ; B i=k and j=ℓ; C i=ℓ and j=k; D i+j=k+ℓ.

Approach
Give stack constructions for the positive choices and a pumping-lemma contradiction for the crossing equalities.

Step-by-step solution
1. For A, scan the string while tracking block order a*b*c*d* in finite control. Maintain the signed difference (#a+#c)−(#b+#d). Store its sign in the finite state and its absolute value as repeated stack symbols. On a or c, add 1; on b or d, subtract 1, popping if this cancels the current sign and pushing otherwise. Accept exactly when the final difference is zero. This is a pushdown automaton, so A is context-free.
2. For D, check block order, push one marker for every a or b, and pop one for every c or d. Reject an attempt to pop an empty counter and accept exactly with zero balance at the end. This enforces i+j=k+ℓ.
3. For C the equalities are nested. The grammar S→aSd|T, T→bTc|ε generates precisely a^i b^j c^j d^i: outer recursion matches a with d, inner recursion matches b with c. Hence C is context-free.
4. For B suppose a context-free pumping length p exists, and take w=a^p b^p c^p d^p. Every decomposition w=uvxyz with |vxy|≤p and |vy|>0 has its pumping window in one block or at most two adjacent blocks. It cannot reach both members of either matching pair a/c or b/d.
5. Pump v and y away, using exponent 0. At least one block count decreases, and its matching nonadjacent block is untouched. The equality i=k or j=ℓ therefore fails. This contradicts the pumping lemma for every allowed decomposition, so B is not context-free.
6. The correct selections are A,C,D. Two independent crossing counters cannot in general be handled by the single last-in-first-out store used for nested matching.
