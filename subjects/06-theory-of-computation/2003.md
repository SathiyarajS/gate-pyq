# GATE 2003: Q15 (printed CS Q15)

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005203452/GATE2003.pdf#page=2), physical page 2. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** D under the standard length-lexicographic convention

## Solution

With an effective shortlex (length-first, then alphabetic) enumeration in increasing order, membership is decided by enumerating until the target appears or an output exceeds it; if the enumerator terminates, absence is also decided. Finite languages are recursive separately. Thus the conventional result is recursive but not necessarily context-free, D; for example{0^n1^n2^n} has a computable ordered enumeration. The reproduction explicitly says lexicographic/alphabetic, not length-first. Ordinary dictionary order can have infinitely many predecessors, so the surpassing argument needs an additional guarantee: enumeration of0,00,000,... never surpasses the absent string1. The conclusion is recorded under the standard effective length-order convention, not justified by that flawed stopping argument for arbitrary dictionary order.

## Solution

The DFA accepts precisely strings that contain two consecutive zeros followed later by a one. With positions 1,4,7 fixed to one, a qualifying pair of zeros must occupy positions 2–3 or 5–6, and the following fixed one guarantees acceptance. Of the 16 assignments to the four free bits, four have the first zero pair, four have the second, and one has both. Inclusion–exclusion gives 4+4−1=7 accepted strings, option C.

## Solution

The grammar generates balanced strings with a as opening bracket and b as closing bracket, including concatenations and the empty word. A deterministic pushdown automaton pushes on a, pops on b, rejects an unmatched b, and accepts at the end exactly when its stack is empty. Thus C is true. The grammar itself is ambiguous, for example ε can be produced directly or through S→SS with two empty children. Its language is closed under concatenation by S→SS and is not regular, since intersection with a*b* gives {aⁿbⁿ:n≥0}.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** A

## Solution

On a nonempty input, q₀ writes 1 in the first cell and moves right in q₁. State q₁ changes any subsequent zeros to ones and moves right until it sees a 1 or the first blank. At either such boundary it moves left into q₀. The cell to its left is now 1, so q₀ moves right again to the boundary in q₁, producing a two-configuration loop. Thus every nonempty binary input diverges, option A. Empty input halts immediately, so option B, whose starred language includes ε, is false.

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** D

## Solution

Let H be the halting language. If L were recursively enumerable, mapping (M,w) to ⟨M,w,1⟩ would make the complement of H recursively enumerable, a contradiction. If the complement of L were recursively enumerable, mapping (M,w) to ⟨M,w,0⟩ would again recognize the complement of H. Therefore neither L nor its complement is recursively enumerable, option D. The tag prevents the union from simply being the universal set.

**Independently derived answer:** B

## Solution

After the flip, the initial left state and the upper-right state are accepting. The empty string is accepted at the initial state. For every nonempty binary string, choose the transition from the initial state to the upper-right state on its first symbol and then use that state’s 0/1 loop for the suffix. Therefore the new language is all of {0,1}*, option B. Flipping final states complements a complete DFA, not a general NFA.
