**Independently derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** Ram gives a polynomial-time reduction from 3-SAT to decision problem Π. Shyam gives a polynomial-time reduction from Π to 3-SAT. What follows? A. Π is NP-hard but not NP-complete; B. Π is in NP but not NP-complete; C. Π is NP-complete; D. Π is neither NP-hard nor in NP.

## Solution

1. Reduction direction matters: 3-SAT≤pΠ means a solver for Π could solve an NP-complete problem, proving hardness. For membership, compute the other reduction and use a satisfying assignment to the resulting 3-CNF as a polynomial certificate. Combining membership and hardness is precisely NP-completeness.

2. The reduction 3-SAT≤pΠ proves Π is NP-hard. The reduction Π≤p 3-SAT supplies a polynomial verifier/NP algorithm because 3-SAT is in NP, so Π is in NP. Together these establish NP-completeness, C, for the standard decision-problem reductions.

**Independently derived answer:** A

**Paraphrased problem context:** Define L={0,1}* if P=NP, and L=∅ otherwise. Which statement is true? A. L is recursive; B. L is recursively enumerable but not recursive; C. L is not recursively enumerable; D. Its recursiveness can be known only after resolving P=NP.

## Solution

1. Reason by cases without trying to resolve the open problem. If P=NP, an always-accept machine decides L. Otherwise an always-reject machine decides L. Each possible language has a halting decider, so the uncertainty about which case holds is not noncomputability of L.

2. Regardless of the truth of P=NP, L is either all binary strings or the empty language. Both are regular and recursive, so L is recursive, A. Establishing that some decider exists does not require knowing which of these two particular deciders describes L.

## Solution

1. For instance, 0010010 splits as 00·100·10 under the given expression. In A it splits into zero-terminated blocks with a final run of ones. 2. Every binary string can be divided into an initial zero run followed by successive 1-and-zero-run blocks, so 0*(10*)*=Σ*. Likewise(1*0)*1* parses successive zero-terminated blocks followed by a final one run and also gives Σ*. Thus A. The other listed expressions do not cover exactly all binary strings.

# GATE 2003: Q15 (printed CS Q15)

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005203452/GATE2003.pdf#page=2), physical page 2. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** D under the standard length-lexicographic convention

**Paraphrased problem context:** If a language’s strings can be effectively enumerated in lexicographic (the reproduction says “alphabetic”) order, which conclusion is intended? A. L is necessarily finite; B. L is regular but not necessarily finite; C. L is context-free but not necessarily regular; D. L is recursive but not necessarily context-free. The distinction between dictionary order and the usual effective length-first order must be addressed.

## Solution

1. Separate two issues: whether membership is decidable, and whether the enumerator itself yields a stopping test. In effective length-first order each target has finitely many predecessors; its absence is detected once the enumeration passes it. Alphabetic dictionary order lacks that finite-predecessor property, so the conventional answer needs the stated interpretation rather than an unqualified appeal to sortedness.

2. With an effective shortlex (length-first, then alphabetic) enumeration in increasing order, membership is decided by enumerating until the target appears or an output exceeds it; if the enumerator terminates, absence is also decided. Finite languages are recursive separately. Thus the conventional result is recursive but not necessarily context-free, D; for example{0^n 1^n 2^n} has a computable ordered enumeration. The reproduction explicitly says lexicographic/alphabetic, not length-first. Ordinary dictionary order can have infinitely many predecessors, so the surpassing argument needs an additional guarantee: enumeration of 0,00,000,... never surpasses the absent string 1. The conclusion is recorded under the standard effective length-order convention, not justified by that flawed stopping argument for arbitrary dictionary order.

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005203452/GATE2003.pdf#page=7), physical page 7. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** A DFA over {0,1} has states q0 (start), q1, q2 and q3 (the only accepting state). Transitions: q0 on 1→q0 and 0→q1; q1 on 1→q0 and 0→q2; q2 on 0→q2 and 1→q3; q3 on either symbol→q3. Let S contain seven-bit strings whose first, fourth and last bits are 1. How many strings of S are accepted?
A. 1  B. 5  C. 7  D. 8

## Solution

1. Reaching q2 requires two consecutive zeros; after reaching q2 a later 1 enters the absorbing accepting state q3.
2. Strings of S have form 1xy1zw1, leaving four free bits and 16 total strings. Adjacent zeros can occur only as xy=00 or zw=00, because fixed 1s separate the blocks.
3. Fixing xy=00 leaves four choices for zw; fixing zw=00 leaves four choices for xy. The string 1001001 is counted twice. Inclusion–exclusion yields 4+4−1=7, so C.

Baseline derivation and caveats (retained): The DFA accepts precisely strings that contain two consecutive zeros followed later by a one. With positions 1,4,7 fixed to one, a qualifying pair of zeros must occupy positions 2–3 or 5–6, and the following fixed one guarantees acceptance. Of the 16 assignments to the four free bits, four have the first zero pair, four have the second, and one has both. Inclusion–exclusion gives 4+4−1=7 accepted strings, option C.

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005203452/GATE2003.pdf#page=7), physical page 7. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** Let G=({S},{a,b},R,S), where R is S→aSb | SS | ε. Which statement is true?
A. G is not ambiguous.
B. There exist x,y in L(G) with xy not in L(G).
C. A deterministic pushdown automaton accepts L(G).
D. A deterministic finite-state automaton accepts L(G).

## Solution

1. Interpret a as an opening bracket and b as a closing bracket. aSb encloses a balanced string, SS concatenates two balanced strings, and ε is balanced. Conversely, split any nonempty balanced string at its first matching close to obtain these productions.
2. A deterministic stack machine pushes on a, pops on b, rejects a pop from an empty stack, and accepts exactly with empty stack at end. This establishes C.
3. A is false: ε has both the direct S→ε tree and S→SS→εε. B is false because S→SS supplies closure under concatenation. D is false because intersecting with regular a*b* leaves the nonregular language {a^n b^n:n≥0}.

Baseline derivation and caveats (retained): The grammar generates balanced strings with a as opening bracket and b as closing bracket, including concatenations and the empty word. A deterministic pushdown automaton pushes on a, pops on b, rejects an unmatched b, and accepts at the end exactly when its stack is empty. Thus C is true. The grammar itself is ambiguous, for example ε can be produced directly or through S→SS with two empty children. Its language is closed under concatenation by S→SS and is not regular, since intersection with a*b* gives {aⁿbⁿ:n≥0}.

**Independently derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** L1 and L2 are languages over Σ. A bijection f:Σ*→Σ* and its inverse are polynomial-time computable, and for every x, x∈L1 iff f(x)∈L2. Which cannot hold?
A. L1∈P and L2 is finite
B. L1∈NP and L2∈P
C. L1 is undecidable and L2 is decidable
D. L1 is recursively enumerable and L2 is recursive
Source caveat: GateForum drops the stars in the displayed domain/codomain; the GFG string-domain version is used here.

## Solution

1. Suppose L2 has a decider D2. An algorithm for L1 computes f(x), which always terminates, then returns D2(f(x)). The membership equivalence makes this algorithm correct and terminating.
2. Thus decidability of L2 forces decidability of L1, contradicting C. In fact the inverse supplies the converse reduction too.
3. The other combinations are not contradictions. Take f to be identity and L1=L2=∅: the language is finite, in P and NP, recursive and recursively enumerable. This single example witnesses the possibility of A, B and D.

Baseline derivation and caveats (retained): If L₂ is decidable, compute f(x) and run its decider; the membership equivalence decides L₁. Hence an undecidable L₁ and decidable L₂ cannot coexist, option C. The other alternatives are possible by choosing finite or otherwise polynomial-time decidable languages: P is contained in NP, and recursively enumerable does not mean nonrecursive. The GFG source specifies f:Σ*→Σ*, which is the interpretation used here; the GateForum retypesetting drops the stars in that displayed domain/codomain.

# GATE 2003: Q53 (printed CS Q53)

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005203452/GATE2003.pdf#page=7), physical page 7. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** A

**Paraphrased problem context:** A single-tape Turing machine has states q0 (start), q1, tape symbols {0,1,B}, and input alphabet {0,1}; B is blank. A transition (q,s,D) means write s, move D, enter q. The table is:
q0 on 0: (q1,1,R); on 1: (q1,1,R); on B: Halt.
q1 on 0: (q1,1,R); on 1: (q0,1,L); on B: (q0,B,L).
Which is true?
A. M does not halt on any string in (0+1)+.
B. M does not halt on any string in (00+1)*.
C. M halts on all strings ending in 0.
D. M halts on all strings ending in 1.
Here + between symbols is union and superscript + means one or more repetitions.

## Solution

1. Empty input starts with a blank under q0 and halts immediately. Since ε belongs to (00+1)*, B cannot be true.
2. On any nonempty input q0 writes a 1 and moves right into q1. q1 passes over zeros, replacing each with 1, until reaching either a preexisting 1 or the right-hand blank.
3. At this boundary q1 moves left to q0. That left cell is now 1, so q0 moves right into q1 without changing it. q1 sees the same boundary symbol and moves left again. These two configurations repeat forever.
4. This applies to every nonempty binary input, regardless of its final symbol. Hence A, not C or D.

Baseline derivation and caveats (retained): On a nonempty input, q₀ writes 1 in the first cell and moves right in q₁. State q₁ changes any subsequent zeros to ones and moves right until it sees a 1 or the first blank. At either such boundary it moves left into q₀. The cell to its left is now 1, so q₀ moves right again to the boundary in q₁, producing a two-configuration loop. Thus every nonempty binary input diverges, option A. Empty input halts immediately, so option B, whose starred language includes ε, is false.

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005203452/GATE2003.pdf#page=7), physical page 7. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** D

**Paraphrased problem context:** Define L0={⟨M,w,0⟩: M halts on w} and L1={⟨M,w,1⟩: M does not halt on w}, where M is a Turing-machine encoding, w a string and the last component a bit. Let L=L0∪L1. Which is true?
A. L is recursively enumerable but its complement is not.
B. Its complement is recursively enumerable but L is not.
C. Both L and its complement are recursive.
D. Neither L nor its complement is recursively enumerable.

## Solution

1. The halting language H is recognizable, but its complement is not recognizable: recognizers for both would yield a decider by dovetailing them.
2. Map each halting-instance pair (M,w) to ⟨M,w,1⟩. It belongs to L exactly when M does not halt. Therefore a recognizer for L would recognize the complement of H, impossible.
3. Map the same pair to ⟨M,w,0⟩. It belongs to the complement of L exactly when M does not halt. A recognizer for that complement would give the same contradiction.
4. Neither tagged language is recognizable, so D. The different tags are essential; halting and nonhalting cases are not simply being unioned without a tag.

Baseline derivation and caveats (retained): Let H be the halting language. If L were recursively enumerable, mapping (M,w) to ⟨M,w,1⟩ would make the complement of H recursively enumerable, a contradiction. If the complement of L were recursively enumerable, mapping (M,w) to ⟨M,w,0⟩ would again recognize the complement of H. Therefore neither L nor its complement is recursively enumerable, option D. The tag prevents the union from simply being the universal set.

# GATE 2003: Q55 (printed CS Q55)

**Question:** [Third-party reproduction](https://media.geeksforgeeks.org/wp-content/cdn-uploads/20211005203452/GATE2003.pdf#page=8), physical page 8. Consult the reproduced wording and choices. Original official provenance and completeness have not been established.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** B

**Paraphrased problem context:** An NFA M over {0,1} has states p (start), q and r (only final). Transitions are p→q on 0 or 1; q→p on 0; q→q on 0 or 1; p→r on 1; q→r on 0; r→q on 0. There are no other transitions. Let L=L(M). M1 is obtained by making r nonfinal and p,q final, and L1=L(M1). Which statement is true?
A. L1={0,1}*−L
B. L1={0,1}*
C. L1⊆L
D. L1=L

## Solution

1. Since p is final in M1, ε is accepted.
2. For any nonempty word, choose p→q on its first symbol. Then choose the 0/1 loop at q for every remaining symbol. This accepting run exists for every word.
3. Therefore L1 is the universal binary language, B. In particular ε∈L1 but ε∉L, ruling out C and D. Since 1∈L as well as L1, they are not complements, ruling out A. NFA acceptance is existential over runs, which is why simply flipping finals fails as a complement construction.

Baseline derivation and caveats (retained): After the flip, the initial left state and the upper-right state are accepting. The empty string is accepted at the initial state. For every nonempty binary string, choose the transition from the initial state to the upper-right state on its first symbol and then use that state’s 0/1 loop for the suffix. Therefore the new language is all of {0,1}*, option B. Flipping final states complements a complete DFA, not a general NFA.
