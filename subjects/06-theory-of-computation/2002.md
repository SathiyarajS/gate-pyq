## Solution

Step 1: Explain both construction and minimality.

Step 2: Label states0,1,2 and send each state to its successor modulo3 for every input symbol. Start and accept at0. To distinguish residues r≠s, append a word of length(3−r) mod3: the state r accepts afterward and s does not. Thus no two residue states can be merged in a deterministic automaton.

Conclusion and qualifications: Use three states for the input length modulo3, advance the residue on every symbol, and accept residue0. The three residues are distinguishable: append a length that brings one chosen residue to0; it does not bring a different residue to0. Thus a minimal DFA has3 states, B, over a nonempty alphabet.

## Solution

Step 1: Separate mathematical closure from multiple-choice wording.

Step 2: A recursive decider halts on every input; reversing its output gives another total decider. A recognizer for an arbitrary RE language need not halt on nonmembers, so merely reversing its states is not a complement recognizer. Option C nevertheless remains true as a weak inclusive disjunction because its first alternative already holds; it is not made false simply by being less informative than A.

Conclusion and qualifications: A decider always halts, so swapping its accept and reject answers decides the complement. Thus A is true. Recursive languages are also recursively enumerable, making C’s printed claim that the complement is either recursive or recursively enumerable true as an inclusive disjunction as well. If C intends recursive or merely-RE-but-not-recursive, its imprecise wording is hiding that distinction. General RE languages are not closed under complement (otherwise dovetailing both recognizers decides every RE language), and CFLs are not closed under complement. A is the strongest precise statement, but the literal single-choice options overlap.

Step 2: A phrase-structure grammar can describe expression and statement shapes while a symbol table checks whether identifier uses agree with their declarations. The latter checks depend on context not captured by merely recognizing those shapes. The answer’s scope is this conventional language-classification question, not a claim that a context-free parser alone proves a C program fully valid or that it decides termination.

Conclusion and qualifications: C includes context-dependent restrictions such as consistent declarations and uses of identifiers, beyond a context-free phrase-structure grammar. The usual classification including such static checks is context-sensitive, B. Runtime behavior and whether a program halts are separate from this classification.

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** M′ halts on x iff M halts and outputs 1 on every input.

## Solution

If M′ halts on x, its simulation finishes regardless of the ignored input w, and M then halts with output 1 for every w. If M′ does not halt on x, M loops on every w, so its description is not in the target language. Thus ⟨M′,x⟩ belongs to HALT iff the computably constructed ⟨M⟩ belongs to {⟨N⟩: N halts and outputs 1 on every input}. A decider for the target language would decide HALT by constructing M and invoking that decider, which is impossible. Hence the target language is undecidable.

q0 --a--> q1 --b--> q2 --b--> q3 (accept), with a,b self-loops at q0.
The loop consumes an arbitrary prefix from (a|b)*, and a nondeterministic branch chooses the a beginning the final abb. Acceptance requires the whole input to end at q3, so the last three letters must be abb. Conversely every string ending in abb has such an accepting path. No epsilon transitions or extra states are needed.

State | a | b
q0 | q1 | q0
q1 | q1 | q2
q2 | q1 | q3
q3 | q1 | q0
For example, reading a after ab or abb leaves suffix a, while reading b after abb leaves no nonempty suffix that begins abb. This invariant proves that q3 is reached at end exactly for strings ending in abb. All transitions are defined and exactly four states are used.
