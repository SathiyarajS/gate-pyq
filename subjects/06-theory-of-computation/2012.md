**Paraphrased problem context:** Assuming P ≠ NP, which of the following is TRUE?
       (A) NP-complete = NP                                  (B) NP-complete ∩ P = ∅
       (C) NP-hard = NP                                      (D) P = NP-complete

## Solution

If an NP-complete language belonged to P, every language in NP could be reduced to it in polynomial time and then decided in polynomial time. This would imply P=NP, contrary to the premise. Thus NP-complete∩P=∅, B. In particular, NP-complete cannot equal NP because P is a nonempty subset of NP.

Worked check / additional reasoning: NP-completeness combines membership in NP with polynomial reductions from every NP language. The contradiction uses both properties: a polynomial algorithm for one complete problem would compose with those reductions to solve all NP problems.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Over Σ={a}, an epsilon-NFA has states s (initial, nonaccepting), t (accepting), and u (nonaccepting). Its only transitions are s --a--> t, t --ε--> u, u --ε--> s. What is the complement of its accepted language within Σ*?
(A) ∅ (B) {ε} (C) a* (D) {a,ε}.

## Solution

The initial state is nonaccepting. A single a reaches the only accepting state, and two epsilon transitions return from that state through the third state to the initial state. Thus each traversal consumes one a and any positive number of a symbols is accepted; epsilon is not. The language is a⁺ and its complement within {a}*=Σ* is {ε}, B.

Worked check / additional reasoning: The initial epsilon-closure is just {s}; no epsilon move leaves s. Therefore the empty input is rejected. After one a reaches t, epsilon moves can return to s, allowing any further a and accepting again at t.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Which problems are decidable? (1) Does a given program ever produce an output? (2) Given a context-free language L, is its complement also context-free? (3) Given a regular language L, is its complement also regular? (4) Given a recursive language L, is its complement also recursive?
(A) 1,2,3,4 (B) 1,2 (C) 2,3,4 (D) 3,4. Complements, shown by overbars in the original, are essential.

## Solution

The overbars in statements 2–4 are essential: each asks about the complement of L. Regular languages are closed under complementation (complete a DFA and exchange accepting/nonaccepting states), and recursive languages are closed under complementation (swap the outcomes of a halting decider), so 3 and 4 always answer yes. Whether an arbitrary program ever outputs is undecidable by simulating a machine and printing only if it halts. For a context-free input language, deciding whether its complement is context-free is an undecidable grammar-property problem; lack of closure alone would not suffice to prove undecidability. Thus the intended decidable entries are 3 and 4, D. For a reduction justifying statement 2, fix a CFL H over a separate alphabet Γ whose complement is the non-CFL {a^k b^k c^k: k≥0}; H is context-free by expressing unequal counts and malformed strings as a union of CFLs. Given a CFG language L over Σ, construct the CFL K consisting of L#Γ*, Σ*#H, and all malformed strings outside Σ*#Γ*. Its complement is (Σ*−L)#(Γ*−H). If L=Σ*, this is empty and context-free. Otherwise, choosing w∉L and intersecting with regular w#Γ* leaves w#(Γ*−H), which is not context-free (remove the fixed prefix by left quotient). Therefore a test for context-freeness of complements would decide the undecidable CFG-universality problem.

Worked check / additional reasoning: A closure fact gives a constant yes-answer for promises 3 and 4, hence a decider exists. For promise 2, nonclosure alone merely says some answers are no; the reduction is necessary to establish that no algorithm can decide all such instances.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Given the language L = {ab, aa, baa}, which of the following strings are in L*?

                    1)   abaabaaabaa
                    2)   aaaabaaaa
                    3)   baaaaabaaaab
                    4)   baaaaabaa
        (A) 1, 2 and 3                                   (B) 2, 3 and 4
        (C) 1, 2 and 4                                   (D) 1, 3 and 4







Q. 26 to Q. 55 carry two marks each.

## Solution

The blocks ab, aa and baa have distinct forced beginnings: at a, the next character decides ab versus aa; at b, only baa is possible. String 1 factors as ab·aa·baa·ab·aa; string 2 as aa·aa·baa·aa; and string 4 as baa·aa·ab·aa. For string 3, baaaaabaaaab, the forced parse is baa·aa·ab·aa·aa followed by a lone b, which is not a block. Its reachable complete-block prefix lengths are 0, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, but its full length is 12. Thus exactly strings 1, 2, 4 belong to L*, C.

Worked check / additional reasoning: Because no listed block is a prefix of another listed block, greedy block recognition cannot discard an alternative valid segmentation. This prefix-code property makes the forced parse failure of string 3 decisive rather than just one unsuccessful guess.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Complete a DFA for binary strings in which every length-three substring has at most two zeros (so 000 is forbidden). All strings shorter than three are accepted. States ε (initial),0,1,00,01,10,11 are accepting; q is rejecting. Existing transitions: ε--0→0, ε--1→1, 0--0→00, 0--1→01, 1--0→10, 1--1→11, 01--0→10, 10--1→01, 11--1→11, q--0,1→q. Which list supplies the missing arcs? Each triple means (source,input,target).
(A) (00,1,00),(00,0,01),(01,1,11),(10,0,00),(11,0,10).
(B) (00,0,01),(00,1,q),(01,1,01),(10,0,11),(11,0,01).
(C) (00,1,01),(00,0,q),(01,1,01),(10,0,10),(11,0,01).
(D) (00,1,01),(00,0,q),(01,1,11),(10,0,00),(11,0,10).

## Solution

The forbidden length-three substring is exactly 000. The two-symbol states retain the last two symbols until the rejecting sink q is reached. Missing transitions are δ(00, 0)=q, δ(00, 1)=01, δ(01, 1)=11, δ(10, 0)=00, and δ(11, 0)=10. These are precisely table D. Every nonsink state accepts, including the states for strings shorter than three symbols.

Worked check / additional reasoning: Appending 0 to suffix 00 creates forbidden 000, requiring the sink. Appending 1 to 00 leaves suffix 01; appending 1 to 01 leaves 11; appending 0 to 10 leaves 00; appending 0 to 11 leaves 10. This derives every missing arc rather than matching only one table entry.
