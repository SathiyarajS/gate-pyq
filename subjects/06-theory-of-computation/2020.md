**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** MTA

**Derived answer:** No correct option; official MTA

**Paraphrased problem context:** Which regular expression denotes binary strings with an odd number of 1s? Here + means union. A ((0+1)*1(0+1)*1)*10*; B (0*10*10*)*0*1; C 10*(0*10*10*)*; D (0*10*10*)*10*. The official answer is MTA, not a letter.

## Solution

Worked explanation

Checking a claimed regular expression needs both inclusions: every generated string must have odd parity, and every odd-parity string must be generated. A violates soundness because the arbitrary (0+1)* portions can introduce extra 1s: use the repeated block 111 and final 1 to generate 1111. B violates completeness on 10, because it ends in a mandatory 1. C violates completeness on 01, because its first symbol is 1. In D, zero repeated blocks give a leading 1; a positive number supplies at least two 1s before the final mandatory one, so 01 is again missing. In the corrected teaching expression 0*(10*10*)*10*, the repeated block contributes pairs of 1s and the final block contributes one; the surrounding zero runs allow arbitrary gaps and ends. This correction is explanatory only and does not replace official MTA scoring.

Derivation and answer

Each offered expression fails. A accepts 1111: choose 111 for one repeated block and the final 1, producing an even number of ones. B always ends in 1 and misses the valid string 10. C begins in 1 and misses 01. D also misses 01: zero repetitions begin with 1, while any positive repetition contributes at least two ones before the final 1. A correct expression is 0*(10*10*)*10*. The official key awards MTA, which is preserved rather than mapped to a letter.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** D

**Derived answer:** D

**Paraphrased problem context:** Test I: if L1∪L2 is regular, both L1 and L2 must be regular. II: regular languages are closed under infinite union. Options: A I only; B II only; C both; D neither.

## Solution

Worked explanation

Closure under a binary operation gives the forward implication: if both languages are regular then their union is regular. It does not give the converse. For a concrete converse counterexample choose L={a^n b^n:n≥0} over {a,b}, and its complement. L is nonregular; its complement is nonregular because otherwise closure under complement would make L regular. Their union is the regular language {a,b}*. For infinite union, each singleton {a^n b^n} has a finite automaton, but the union over all n is L. Finite union closure cannot be extended to countably many automata by treating an infinite construction as a finite automaton.

Derivation and answer

For I, take a nonregular language L and its complement; their union isΣ*, regular, although neither component is regular. For II, each singleton{a^n b^n} is regular but their union over n≥0 is not. Both assertions are false, D.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** C

**Derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** L={a^n:n≥0}∪{a^n b^n:n≥0}. Test I L is deterministic context-free; II L is context-free but not deterministic context-free; III L is not LL(k) for any k. Options: A I only; B II only; C I and III; D III only.

## Solution

Worked explanation

A deterministic pushdown recognizer can use an accepting a-phase, initially with a bottom marker. Each a pushes one marker. Seeing the first b changes permanently to a b-phase and pops one marker per b; a b with no marker, or any later a, rejects. At end-of-input it accepts either the a-phase (including ε) or the b-phase with exactly the bottom marker left. This establishes I constructively and rejects II. The familiar non-LL(k) separation is a language-level result: do not infer it merely because the obvious grammar S→A|B, A→aA|ε, B→aBb|ε has a FIRST-set conflict. The shared-prefix argument below explains the obstruction; a full formal proof that no equivalent LL(k) grammar exists requires the bounded-lookahead language theorem, not just testing that one grammar. III is the standard classification used by the official answer.

Derivation and answer

A DPDA pushes while reading a symbols, then pops one per b after the first b. It accepts an all-a input by final state at end-of-input, and accepts an a^n b^n input when the count matches, so the language is deterministic context-free. The standard strict-separation example a* union {a^n b^n} is not an LL(k) language for any finite k: arbitrarily long common a-prefixes postpone the distinction between an unmatched all-a ending and the matching b-suffix beyond any fixed lookahead. A predictive leftmost derivation cannot use that late distinction to discharge an unbounded number of already predicted matching b obligations. This is the language-level bounded-lookahead obstruction, not merely an observation that one displayed grammar is not left-factored. Thus I and III, C.

**Official answer:** A

**Derived answer:** A

**Paraphrased problem context:** Which languages of machine encodings are undecidable? L1={⟨M⟩:L(M)=∅}; L2={⟨M,w,q⟩:M on w reaches q in exactly 100 steps}; L3={⟨M⟩:L(M) is not recursive}; L4={⟨M⟩:L(M) has at least 21 members}. A L1,L3,L4; B L1,L3; C L2,L3; D L2,L3,L4.

## Solution

Worked explanation

Apply Rice’s theorem only to semantic properties of the recognized language that are neither true for every recognizable language nor false for all of them. Emptiness is nontrivial (∅ versus {ε}); nonrecursiveness is nontrivial (a recognizable undecidable language versus a finite language); at least 21 strings is nontrivial (∅ versus any 21-element finite language). Thus L1,L3,L4 qualify. L2 instead concerns a bounded computation trace. A simulator executes at most 100 transitions and checks q at the specified step, rejecting if the run ends earlier without that event. This always halts. An unbounded reachability question would be very different.

Derivation and answer

TM-language emptiness, being nonrecursive, and having at least 21 strings are each nontrivial semantic properties of recursively enumerable languages, so Rice’s theorem makes L1, L3, L4 undecidable. L2 is decidable by simulating the specified machine and input for exactly 100 steps and checking the state. Thus A.

**Official answer:** A

**Derived answer:** A

**Paraphrased problem context:** L1={wxyx:w,x,y∈{0,1}*}; L2={xy:x,y∈{a,b}*, |x|=|y|, x≠y}. Which statement is true? A L1 regular and L2 context-free; B L1 context-free but not regular, L2 context-free; C neither context-free; D L1 context-free, L2 not context-free.

## Solution

Worked explanation

Empty strings are permitted for the starred variables in L1. Setting x=y=ε produces every binary w, so repeated appearances of x impose no restriction at all. For L2, the grammar below is more informative than incorrectly invoking closure under complement of context-free languages. Write V→b|aVa|aVb|bVa|bVb in addition to the displayed U productions. U and V wrap arbitrary equal numbers of symbols around different central letters. Concatenating them makes the central positions differ by half the total length. Conversely, if unequal halves of length m differ at position r, choose first component length 2r−1 and second length 2(m−r)+1. Their centers land at r and m+r. This proves both grammar inclusions, hence context-freeness without claiming that arbitrary complements of CFLs are CFLs.

Derivation and answer

For L1 choose x=ε, y=ε and let w be any binary string; hence L1=Σ*, regular. For L2 a CFG can guess a differing position in the two equal-length halves. Explicitly let U generate all odd-length strings with central a via U→a|aUa|aUb|bUa|bUb, and V generate those with central b by the analogous rules with base b. S→UV|VU then generates exactly even-length strings whose two halves differ somewhere. If component lengths are 2i+1 and 2j+1, their differing central symbols are i+j+1 positions apart, exactly half the total length. Conversely any mismatch supplies such a split. Thus L2 is context-free and A holds.

**Official answer:** 6 to 6

**Derived answer:** 6

**Paraphrased problem context:** L consists of strings over {a,b} whose number of a symbols is divisible by 2 but not by 3. Find the minimum states in a DFA for L.

## Solution

Worked explanation

DFA specification: states q0,…,q5; start q0; accepting q2,q4; δ(qr,a)=q((r+1) mod 6); δ(qr,b)=qr. This is a complete transition graph, with an a-cycle of length six and a b-self-loop at each vertex. Counting zero a symbols is divisible by both 2 and 3, so q0 correctly rejects. For minimality, all qr are reached by a^r. The accepting suffix exponents modulo 6 for q0…q5 are respectively {2,4},{1,3},{0,2},{1,5},{0,4},{3,5}. These six sets differ pairwise. Choose an exponent in the symmetric difference to distinguish any two states, proving six inequivalent prefixes and the lower bound.

Derivation and answer

Track the number of a symbols modulo 6. On a increment the remainder, on b leave it unchanged, and accept remainders 2 and 4; these are exactly even counts not divisible by 3. All six states are reachable. From remainder r, accepting suffix lengths modulo 6 form {2-r, 4-r}. These six sets are distinct, so any pair of remainders is distinguished by some suffix a^k. Myhill-Nerode therefore requires all 6 states, matching the construction.
