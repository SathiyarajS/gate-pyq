**Official answer:** 0.125 to 0.125

**Derived answer:** 0.125

**Paraphrased problem context:** Choose uniformly from all binary relations on {1,2,3}. What is the probability the relation is reflexive, to three decimal places?

## Solution

Worked explanation

A binary relation is any subset of A×A. With |A|=3 there are nine ordered pairs, not three unordered pairs. Every pair can be included or omitted independently, yielding 512 subsets. Reflexivity requires (1,1),(2,2),(3,3) to be present; the other six bits remain free, yielding 64 favorable relations. The ratio is 64/512=0.125. Equivalently the three required diagonal bits each have probability 1/2, so their conjunction has probability (1/2)³. Symmetry and transitivity are not required.

Derivation and answer

There are2⁹ binary relations on a three-element set. Reflexivity fixes the three diagonal pairs as present, leaving six arbitrary choices and 2⁶ relations. Under uniform random selection the probability is 2⁶/2⁹=1/8=0.125.

**Official answer:** 7 to 7

**Paraphrased problem context:** G is a group of 35 elements. What is the largest possible order of a subgroup other than G itself?

## Solution

Worked explanation

Lagrange’s theorem gives an upper-bound filter: subgroup order must divide the group order. The divisors of 35 are 1,5,7,35, and the last is excluded because a subgroup of full finite order is G. Divisibility alone would not guarantee existence of every divisor-sized subgroup in arbitrary finite groups. Here the largest remaining divisor is prime, so Cauchy’s theorem supplies an element of order 7, and its generated cyclic subgroup has exactly 7 elements. Hence the upper bound is attained for every group of order 35, not just for one cyclic example.

Derivation and answer

By Lagrange’s theorem a proper subgroup order divides35 and is smaller than35, so it is 1, 5, or7. Cauchy’s theorem guarantees an element of order7, whose cyclic subgroup has 7 elements. Thus the largest possible proper subgroup size is 7.

**Paraphrased problem context:** W has no free x. Which equivalence is NOT valid (usual nonempty domain)? A ∀x(p(x)∨W) ≡ (∀x p(x))∨W; B ∃x(p(x)∧W) ≡ (∃x p(x))∧W; C ∀x(p(x)→W) ≡ (∀x p(x))→W; D ∃x(p(x)→W) ≡ (∀x p(x))→W.

## Solution

Worked explanation

Rewrite implication first: p→W is ¬p∨W. Universal quantification then gives ∀x(¬p(x)∨W)≡(∀x¬p(x))∨W≡¬∃x p(x)∨W, which is (∃x p(x))→W, not the universal antecedent printed in C. A countermodel makes the failure tangible: p(u)=true,p(v)=false,W=false. C’s left side fails at u; its right side is true because ∀x p(x) is false. For D, existential quantification instead yields (∃x¬p(x))∨W≡¬∀x p(x)∨W, exactly the printed right side. The side condition that W has no free x is essential when moving W outside a quantifier.

Derivation and answer

Let the domain have two elements, let p be true at one and false at the other, and let W be false. Then ∀x(p(x)→W) is false, while(∀x p(x))→W is true because its antecedent is false. Thus C is not valid. A andB pull an x-independent formula across the corresponding quantifier. D follows from ∃x(¬p(x)∨W)≡¬∀x p(x)∨W in the standard nonempty-domain semantics.

**Official answer:** 12 to 12

**Derived answer:** 12

**Paraphrased problem context:** How many distinct permutations of LILAC place no character in any of its original positions, treating the two Ls as indistinguishable?

## Solution

Worked explanation

Number positions 1…5 with original symbols L,I,L,A,C. Neither L may occupy 1 or 3, so choose their two positions from {2,4,5} in C(3,2)=3 ways. After that, remaining positions are 1,3 and one r in {2,4,5}. At positions 1 and 3 any of I,A,C is allowed because the original symbol there was L. The only forbidden placement is the symbol originally at r returning to r. There are 3!=6 arrangements of I,A,C, and fixing that forbidden symbol leaves 2!=2 bad arrangements. Therefore each L choice yields 4 valid arrangements, total 12. Treating the Ls as labeled would double-count; using !5 blindly also misses their shared exclusion from both original L slots.

Derivation and answer

The two indistinguishable Ls must occupy two of positions2, 4, 5, giving three choices. The one unoccupied position among those three is the original location of one of I, A, C. Place I, A, C in that position and positions1, 3: of the 6 permutations, exactly2 put that particular character back in its original position. Thus each L placement permits4 arrangements, totaling3*4=12. Direct enumeration of the 60 distinct permutations also yields12.

**Official answer:** 0.5 to 0.5

**Derived answer:** 0.5

**Paraphrased problem context:** For n>2, a is a fixed nonzero vector in {0,1}^n and x is uniform in {0,1}^n. What is the probability that Σ a_i x_i is odd?

## Solution

Worked explanation

View the sum modulo 2. Since a is nonzero, at least one coordinate j has coefficient 1. Fix every random bit except x_j; those fixed bits determine some parity c. The remaining sum parity is c⊕x_j. A uniform x_j makes that result equally likely to be 0 or 1, regardless of c. Averaging over all choices of the other bits preserves probability 1/2. The pairing proof below is the equivalent combinatorial version. The nonzero hypothesis matters: if a were the zero vector, the sum would always be 0 and the probability would be 0.

Derivation and answer

Because a is nonzero, choose an index j with a_j=1. Pair each vector x with the vector obtained by flipping only x_j. This is a fixed-point-free bijection on all2^n equally likely inputs, and it reverses the parity of the dot product. Exactly half of the inputs yield an odd sum, so the probability is 1/2. The argument in fact works for any n≥1 with nonzero a.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Start with complete bipartite K3,4, add a vertex s adjacent to all seven original vertices, and keep all original edges. What is the minimum number of edge colors? Edges sharing an endpoint must differ.

## Solution

Worked explanation

A degree-d vertex forces d distinct colors on its incident edges. Vertex s has degree 7, while original vertices have degree 5 or 4, so Δ=7 supplies the lower bound. Do not apply the bipartite edge-coloring theorem directly: adding s creates triangles. Instead verify the constructive coloring below. Its ai-bj matrix rows are [2,3,4,5], [1,4,3,6], [5,1,2,4]. Row colors are distinct and omit the corresponding s-ai color 1,2,3. Columns are [2,1,5],[3,4,1],[4,3,2],[5,6,4]; each is distinct and omits its s-bj color 4,5,6,7 respectively. At s all seven incident colors differ. Every pair of adjacent edges is therefore checked, proving seven colors suffice.

Derivation and answer

The new vertex s has degree7, so at least7 edge colors are needed. A concrete7-coloring proves sufficiency. Call the parts a 0, a 1, a 2 and b0, b1, b2, b3. Give edges s-a 0, s-a 1, s-a 2 colors1, 2, 3, and s-b0,..., s-b3 colors4, 5, 6, 7. For the three rows of edges ai-bj, use colors (2, 3, 4, 5), (1, 4, 3, 6), and (5, 1, 2, 4), respectively. Each row and column has distinct colors and avoids its endpoint edge to s. Thus this is a proper edge-coloring with 7 colors, proving the minimum7.
