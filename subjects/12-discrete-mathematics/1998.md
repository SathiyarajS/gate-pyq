**Independently derived answer:** B: 3/8

**Essential problem context (paraphrased):** A fair die is rolled independently three times. Find probability of exactly one odd result among 1/6, 3/8, 1/8, 1/2.

## Solution

Each roll is odd with probability 3/6=1/2 and even with probability 1/2. Choose the unique odd roll in three ways. For each choice, independence gives probability (1/2)^3=1/8. The three cases are disjoint, so their total is 3/8. Counting only one ordering would give 1/8 and omit the other positions. This calculation uses a fair die and independent rolls, the conventional premises.

## Solution

Intersection preserves reflexivity because each diagonal pair belongs to both; it preserves symmetry and transitivity because the required reversed or composed pair belongs to each relation. Thus intersection is always an equivalence, whereas union is not necessarily one, giving C. Some particular unions are equivalences; the question asks a general guarantee.

**Independently derived answer:** 25/36, assuming independent uniform arrival times.

**Essential problem context (paraphrased):** Two friends arrive between 4 and 5 p.m. Each waits ten minutes or until 5 p.m., whichever comes first. Find the probability they do not meet.

## Solution

Let X and Y be their arrival times in minutes after 4 p.m. The usual intended probability model is that X and Y are independent and uniformly distributed on [0,60]. The problem does not explicitly supply this distribution; a numerical probability requires it.

They meet precisely when |X−Y|≤10, apart from a zero-probability issue about departure at the exact arrival instant. Truncating the wait at 5 p.m. does not change this condition: both people arrive by then, so the earlier arrival must still be present if the gap is at most ten minutes.

All pairs (X,Y) are equally likely in a 60-by-60 square of area 3600. Failure to meet is the union of the regions Y>X+10 and X>Y+10. Each is a right triangle with legs 50 and area 50²/2. Hence P(no meeting)=2(50²/2)/60²=2500/3600=25/36. As a check, the complementary meeting probability is 11/36. The answer is not 5/6: that treats one person's ten-minute window as if it were the whole two-dimensional event. Nonuniform or correlated arrivals can give a different answer.

**Independently derived answer:** D_n=n(n−3)/2 for n≥3.

**Essential problem context (paraphrased):** Prove by induction that an n-sided polygon has n(n−3)/2 diagonals.

## Solution

A diagonal joins two nonadjacent vertices; count all such vertex pairs, regardless of where segments lie for a nonconvex polygon. Let D_n be their number. A triangle has no diagonals, and 3(3−3)/2=0, establishing the base case.

Assume D_n=n(n−3)/2 for an n-gon. Insert a new vertex v between two formerly adjacent vertices u and w, replacing the boundary edge uw by the two boundary edges uv and vw. All old diagonals remain diagonals. The former edge uw becomes one additional diagonal. The new vertex v can be joined by diagonals to the n−2 old vertices other than its two neighbors u and w. Thus the increase is 1+(n−2)=n−1, not merely n−2.

Therefore D_(n+1)=D_n+n−1=n(n−3)/2+n−1=(n²−n−2)/2=(n+1)(n−2)/2. This is the required formula with n replaced by n+1, completing induction.

As a consistency check, choosing any pair of vertices gives n(n−1)/2 segments; subtract the n boundary edges to obtain n(n−3)/2. For n=4 the count is 2 and for n=5 it is 5, agreeing with the recurrence. The insertion argument's old-edge contribution is the usual subtle point in this induction.

**Essential problem context (paraphrased):** R consists of directed cycles a→b→c→a and d→e→f→g→h→d. Find the smallest integers m<n with R^m=R^n.

## Solution

Each vertex has exactly one outgoing edge, so R is a permutation: a 3-cycle and a disjoint 5-cycle. R^t sends each vertex t positions forward around its component, because composition corresponds to a path of length t.

On the 3-cycle, R^m and R^n agree exactly when n−m is divisible by 3. On the 5-cycle, they agree exactly when n−m is divisible by 5. Both requirements hold precisely when 15=lcm(3,5) divides n−m. Necessity follows by inspecting even one vertex in each component; sufficiency follows because a multiple of a cycle's length returns every vertex to its starting position.

Thus R^15 is the identity relation on the eight vertices, and R^16=R. If powers are numbered from 1, the smallest first exponent is m=1 and the smallest later matching exponent is n=16. If the standard zeroth power R^0=I is permitted, the earliest pair is instead (0,15). The reproduced wording says “integers” without explicitly restricting them to positive powers, so both conventions must be disclosed.

If one extends this permutation relation to negative powers and allows all integers, there is no smallest pair: (m,m+15) works for arbitrarily negative m. Ordinary relation-power questions use nonnegative or positive exponents.

It is incorrect to use 3+5=8 as the period: both components must return simultaneously, which requires a common multiple rather than a sum. Neither cycle contains a self-loop, and there are no cross-component edges; adding either would change the relation being analyzed.

**Essential problem context (paraphrased):** For A={a,b,c,d} and partition Π1={{a,b,c},{d}}, list the induced equivalence relation.

## Solution

The equivalence relation induced by a partition relates x to y exactly when they belong to the same block. Therefore take the Cartesian square of each block and unite the results:

R=({a,b,c}×{a,b,c}) ∪ ({d}×{d})
 ={(a,a),(a,b),(a,c),
   (b,a),(b,b),(b,c),
   (c,a),(c,b),(c,c),(d,d)}.

There are 3²+1²=10 ordered pairs. In particular, (a,b) and (b,a) are different pairs and both must appear. The isolated singleton block still contributes (d,d); reflexivity does not disappear for a singleton. No pair with one endpoint d and the other in {a,b,c} is present, because that would merge the two equivalence classes.

Reflexivity follows because each element shares its block with itself. Symmetry follows because “in the same block” is symmetric. Transitivity follows because if x shares a block with y and y shares a block with z, disjointness of partition blocks forces all three into that one block. Thus the listed relation indeed has exactly the required equivalence classes.

a*c=a*(a*b*a)=(a*a)*b*a=a*b*a=c,

a*b*c
 = a*b*(c*a*c)
 = [a*(b*c)*a]*c

The last equality uses the same sandwich identity with x=a and y=b*c, which is an element of A by closure. Every step preserves the order of factors, so no forbidden commutativity assumption is being made.

This proves the desired middle-factor elimination for arbitrary a,b,c. Iterating it shows any product of at least two factors reduces to its first factor multiplied by its last. The identity is compatible with noncommutativity; for example the left-zero operation x*y=x is associative and satisfies the premise because x*y=x and y*x=y differ whenever x≠y. Thus the proof does not imply that all elements of A are equal or that the operation is commutative.
