**Paraphrased problem context:** For an integer x≥2, define p: x belongs to {8,9,10,11,12}; q: x is composite; r: x is a perfect square; s: x is prime. Find x satisfying NOT((p implies q) AND (NOT r OR NOT s)).

## Solution

De Morgan gives ¬(p→q)∨(r∧s)=(p∧¬q)∨(r∧s). No integer≥2 is both a perfect square and prime, so the second term is false. Among 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 only 11 is not composite. Therefore x=11.

First replace the implication p⇒q by ¬p∨q. Negating the outside conjunction produces (p∧¬q)∨(r∧s), so only two kinds of integer could qualify. A square x=k^2 with x≥2 has k≥2 and a proper divisor k; it cannot also be prime. Consequently x must lie in the listed set while failing compositeness. Checking that set leaves exactly 11, and substituting 11 makes the original inner conjunction false as required.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Let a_n count length-n binary strings with no consecutive 1s. Which recurrence holds? A: a_n=a_(n−1)+2a_(n−2). B: a_n=a_(n−1)+a_(n−2). C: a_n=2a_(n−1)+a_(n−2). D: a_n=2a_(n−1)+2a_(n−2).

## Solution

Partition valid n-bit strings by their last symbol. Those ending 0 append 0 to any valid (n−1)-bit string. Those ending 1 must end 01 and append 01 to a valid (n−2)-bit string. Hence a_n=a_(n−1)+a_(n−2), B, with a_0=1, a_1=2.

The two cases must be disjoint and exhaustive. For n≥2, a valid string ends either in 0, or in 01; the latter is forced because a final 1 cannot follow another 1. Removing those suffixes gives arbitrary valid shorter strings and is reversible, so there is neither overcounting nor omission. For example a_2=3 corresponds to 00,01,10, matching a_1+a_0=2+1. Initial values accompany the recurrence even though only the recurrence is requested.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Find the coefficient of x^12 in (x^3+x^4+x^5+x^6+...)^3.

## Solution

Factor the series as x³/(1−x). Its cube is x⁹(1−x)^−3, so the coefficient of x¹² equals that of x³ in(1−x)^−3, namely C(5, 2)=10. Equivalently distribute three excess units across three exponents, each originally at least 3.

Each of the three factors contributes one exponent at least 3. Write those exponents as 3+i,3+j,3+k with i,j,k≥0. To total 12, their excesses must satisfy i+j+k=3. Stars-and-bars counts C(3+3−1,3−1)=10 solutions. The three factors are distinguishable, so choices such as(3,4,5) and(4,3,5) contribute separately; counting unordered partitions would undercount the coefficient.

**Paraphrased problem context:** A function f from positive integers to positive integers satisfies f(n)=f(n/2) for even n and f(n)=f(n+5) for odd n. If R is its set of attained values, what is the maximum possible size of R?

## Solution

For even n halve n. For odd n>5, applying the odd rule and then halving sends n to(n+5)/2<n. Thus every value eventually reaches 1..5. The numbers 1, 2, 3, 4 are connected by the cycle 1→6→3→8→4→2→1, while 5→10→5 forms another component. Divisibility by 5 is invariant under both rule equalities, so these components never merge. A function constant 1 on nonmultiples of 5 and constant 2 on multiples satisfies both rules and realizes two values. Therefore the maximum range size is 2.

The equations force f to be constant on connected components generated by the permitted transformations. Descent shows there are at most two components, while the divisibility-by 5 invariant proves that they cannot merge. For an odd n,5 divides n+5 exactly when it divides n; for an even n, halving preserves divisibility by 5 because 2 is invertible modulo 5. Assigning two different positive values to these two classes supplies an explicit function attaining the upper bound, so this is a maximum rather than just an estimate.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Repeatedly toss a fair coin twice. TH outputs Y and stops; HH or HT outputs N and stops; TT restarts the experiment. Find the probability of eventually outputting Y, to two decimal places.

## Solution

Let p be the probability of eventual Y. A first-round TH gives Y with probability 1/4, while TT with probability 1/4 restarts independently. Thus p=1/4+p/4, so p=1/3. Infinite restart has probability lim(1/4)^n=0. Two-decimal answer 0.33.

Another derivation conditions on the first round that does not restart. Of the equally likely terminal outcomes TH,HH,HT, exactly one produces Y, giving 1/3. Or sum the disjoint cases: Y after k restarts has probability(1/4)^k(1/4), and their geometric series is(1/4)/(1−1/4). The single-round probability 1/4 is not the final answer because TT does not end the experiment in failure.
