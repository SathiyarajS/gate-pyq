**Paraphrased problem context:** Consider the following logical inferences.

       I1: If it rains then the cricket match will not be played.
           The cricket match was played.
           Inference: There was no rain.
       I2: If it rains then the cricket match will not be played.
           It did not rain.
           Inference: The cricket match was played.

       Which of the following is TRUE?
       (A) Both I1 and I2 are correct inferences
       (B) I1 is correct but I2 is not a correct inference
       (C) I1 is not correct but I2 is a correct inference
       (D) Both I1 and I2 are not correct inferences

## Solution

Let R mean rain and M mean match played. The premise is R→¬M. Its contrapositive M→¬R proves inference I1. From ¬R alone one cannot infer M: the match could be cancelled for another reason. I2 denies the antecedent and is invalid. Hence B.

Worked check / additional reasoning: A truth assignment supplies a counterexample to I2: set R=false and M=false. Both R→¬M and ¬R are true, but M is false. In contrast, R=true together with M=true would violate the conditional, so I1 is forced.

**Paraphrased problem context:** What is the correct translation of the following statement into mathematical logic?
                                           “Some real numbers are rational”
        (A) ∃x (real(x) ∨ rational(x))
        (B) ∀x (real(x) → rational(x))
        (C) ∃x (real(x) ∧ rational(x))
        (D) ∃x (rational(x) → real(x))

## Solution

Some asserts existence of an object satisfying both properties. The translation is ∃x(real(x)∧rational(x)), C. A disjunction need not satisfy both properties, a universal quantifier says all rather than some, and an implication can be true solely because its antecedent is false.

Worked check / additional reasoning: The conjunction ensures that the same witness x is both real and rational. For instance, x=1/2 is such a witness. Using ∨ would allow an object satisfying just one property and would not express the stated overlap.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Let G be a simple undirected planar graph on 10 vertices with 15 edges. If G is a connected graph,
        then the number of bounded faces in any embedding of G on the plane is equal to
        (A) 3                   (B) 4                    (C) 5                   (D) 6

## Solution

Euler’s formula for a connected plane graph is V−E+F=2, counting the unbounded outer face. With V=10 and E=15, F=7. Exactly one face is unbounded, so the number of bounded faces is 6, D. The count is independent of the particular embedding.

Worked check / additional reasoning: F=E−V+2=15−10+2=7 is the total face count. Removing the exterior gives E−V+1=6. Using seven as the bounded count would mistakenly count the outside region as a bounded face.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Consider a random variable X that takes values +1 and −1 with probability 0.5 each. The values of
        the cumulative distribution function F(x) at x = −1 and +1 are
        (A) 0 and 0.5           (B) 0 and 1              (C) 0.5 and 1           (D) 0.25 and 0.75

## Solution

The cumulative distribution is F(x)=P(X≤x). At x=−1 it includes the mass at −1, giving 0.5. At x=+1 it includes both possible outcomes, giving 1. The requested pair is (0.5, 1), C.

Worked check / additional reasoning: The full CDF is 0 for x<−1, 1/2 for −1≤x<1, and 1 for x≥1. CDFs include the probability mass at their argument; using P(X<x) instead would produce incorrect endpoint values.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Which candidate graph is isomorphic to the given graph? All graphs are simple and undirected. The following edge lists faithfully encode the unlabeled drawings; labels are assigned only for this transcription. Given: {01,12,23,34,40,05,06,67}, i.e. a five-cycle with one-edge and two-edge branches attached at vertex 0.
(A) {01,12,20,23,04,45,06,67}.
(B) {01,12,23,34,40,05,06,67}, drawn with a curved edge completing its cycle.
(C) {01,12,23,34,40,15,06,67}.
(D) {01,12,23,30,04,45,06,67}. Each two-digit pair denotes one undirected edge between its digits.

## Solution

The given graph consists of one five-vertex cycle, with a pendant edge and a two-edge pendant path attached to the same cycle vertex. That attachment vertex has degree four; two vertices have degree one and the other five have degree two. Option B has exactly this structure: its curved outer edge completes the five-cycle, and its central degree-four vertex supports the one-edge upward branch and the two-edge lower-right branch. Map the attachment vertex, then the two distinct-length branches, then the five-cycle in order to obtain an isomorphism. A has a triangle and D a four-cycle; C attaches its pendant branches at different cycle vertices. Hence B.

Worked check / additional reasoning: An isomorphism preserves cycle lengths, degrees, and which cycle vertices carry branches. Degree sequence alone is not sufficient: candidate C has a different attachment pattern. In the supplied edge encoding, the identity map from the given labels to B demonstrates every edge correspondence.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Suppose a fair six-sided die is rolled once. If the value on the die is 1, 2, or 3, the die is rolled a
        second time. What is the probability that the sum total of values that turn up is at least 6?
        (A) 10/21               (B) 5/12                     (C) 2/3                (D) 1/6

## Solution

If the first roll is 6, success occurs immediately with probability 1/6; first rolls 4 or 5 stop unsuccessfully. For first roll 1, the second must be 5 or 6 (two outcomes); for 2 it must be 4, 5, 6 (three); for 3 it must be 3, 4, 5, 6 (four). Each ordered two-roll result has probability 1/36. Total success probability is 1/6+(2+3+4)/36=15/36=5/12, B. One-roll and two-roll terminal outcomes are not equiprobable.

Worked check / additional reasoning: Conditioning on the first roll gives (1/6)(2/6)+(1/6)(3/6)+(1/6)(4/6)+1/6. The second roll is not performed after 4,5,6, so treating all terminal outcomes as one uniform list gives the wrong denominator.

**Paraphrased problem context:** How many onto (surjective) functions exist from an n-element set, n≥2, to a two-element set?
(A) 2^n (B) 2^n−1 (C) 2^n−2 (D) 2(2^n−2).

## Solution

Each of n domain elements has two choices, giving 2^n functions. A function is not onto precisely when all n elements map to one fixed codomain element. There are two such constant functions. Subtract them to obtain 2^n−2 surjections, C.

Worked check / additional reasoning: For n=2 the four functions are the two constants and the two bijections, leaving two onto functions. The formula 2²−2=2 passes this small-case check and explains why subtracting just one constant function is insufficient.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Let G be a complete undirected graph on 6 vertices. If vertices of G are labeled, then the number of
        distinct cycles of length 4 in G is equal to
        (A) 15                 (B) 30                   (C) 90              (D) 360

## Solution

Choose the four vertices in C(6, 4)=15 ways. On a chosen set, cyclic rotations and reversal represent the same undirected cycle, so there are 4!/(4×2)=3 distinct cycles. The total is 15×3=45. None of the listed 15, 30, 90, 360 equals 45. Counting opposite orientations separately would give 90, but that is not the usual convention for distinct undirected cycles. The scoring key says Marks to All. The absent standard answer is an independent diagnosis, not an attributed official reason.

Worked check / additional reasoning: For four fixed labels a,b,c,d, the three undirected cycles can be represented as a-b-c-d-a, a-b-d-c-a, and a-c-b-d-a. Fixing the first vertex removes rotations, and identifying each traversal with its reverse removes the factor two.
