

# GATE 2023 CS: Q15

**Question:** [Official archived paper page 12](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2023/cs_2023.pdf#page=12); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** A

**Derived answer:** A

**Paraphrased problem context:** The Lucas sequence has L1=1,L2=3 and Ln=L(n−1)+L(n−2). Put α=(1+√5)/2, β=(1−√5)/2, γ=(1−√5)/3. Which is Ln? A αⁿ+βⁿ; B αⁿ−γⁿ; C αⁿ+γⁿ; D αⁿ−βⁿ.

## Solution

The characteristic roots of r²=r+1 are α=(1+√5)/2 and β=(1−√5)/2. The sequence αⁿ+βⁿ satisfies the recurrence; at n=1 it is 1, and at n=2 it is(α+β)²−2αβ=1+2=3. These initial values uniquely specify the sequence, so A. The plus sign and both denominators2 were visually checked.

Teaching detail. For a recurrence Ln=L(n−1)+L(n−2), trying a solution rⁿ gives r²−r−1=0. Each root therefore yields a valid exponential sequence, and linear combinations remain solutions. Here α+β=1 and αβ=−1 establish the initial values without numerical square-root calculations. Uniqueness follows inductively: if two sequences agree at positions 1 and 2, their recurrence makes them agree at 3 and then at every later position. Checking only one initial value would not establish the result.

# GATE 2023 CS: Q26

**Question:** [Official archived paper page 24](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2023/cs_2023.pdf#page=24); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** B, C

**Derived answer:** B;C

**Paraphrased problem context:** Over integers, the target conjecture is ∀x(P(x)⇒∃y Q(x,y)). Which imply it? A ∃x(P(x)∧∀y Q(x,y)); B ∀x∀y Q(x,y); C ∃y∀x(P(x)⇒Q(x,y)); D ∃x(P(x)∧∃y Q(x,y)).

## Solution

B states Q(x, y) for every pair, so each x satisfying P certainly has a witness y (the integer domain is nonempty). C provides one y working for every qualifying x, a stronger condition than allowing the witness to depend on x. A, D only give information for some x. For a common counterexample take P true at 0 and 1 and Q(x, y) true exactly when x=0: A, D hold, but the conjecture fails at 1. Thus B, C; quantifier scopes were visually checked.

Teaching detail. To prove the target, start with an arbitrary integer x satisfying P(x) and find a witness y. Under B, every integer y works. Under C, the single promised y works for this x and all other qualifying x. A and D describe only one favorable x, leaving other integers uncontrolled. In the counterexample where P holds at 0 and 1 but Q holds only for first coordinate 0, choosing x=0 makes both existential options true while x=1 defeats the target conjecture.

# GATE 2023 CS: Q30

**Question:** [Official archived paper page 28](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2023/cs_2023.pdf#page=28); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** NAT, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** 2 to 2 OR 4 to 4

**Derived answer:** 2 or4, according to the self-loop adjacency convention

**Paraphrased problem context:** An undirected graph on {1,2,3,4,5} has edges {1,2},{2,3},{2,4},{3,5},{4,5}, and self-loops at 3 and 4. Find the sum of the five eigenvalues of its adjacency matrix. Account for the two officially accepted self-loop conventions.

## Solution

The sum of eigenvalues equals trace(A). Only the self-loops at vertices3 and 4 contribute diagonal entries; the other edges do not. If each loop contributes1 to its adjacency diagonal, the trace is 2. If the undirected-loop convention records2 on the diagonal to count incidences, the trace is 4. Both exact alternatives are officially accepted: 2 to 2 OR 4 to 4. This is a union of two singleton answers, not the interval from 2 to 4. The two loops were visually inspected.

Teaching detail. The characteristic polynomial coefficient for the sum of eigenvalues gives trace(A), counting algebraic multiplicity, even without computing individual eigenvalues. Every edge joining two different vertices contributes off-diagonal entries and cannot affect this trace. The only contributions are therefore the two loops. Some adjacency conventions count each loop once, others twice to preserve row-sum degree identities. The official key accepts exactly 2 or exactly 4; 3 is not accepted, despite lying numerically between them. This is why the key must retain separate singleton ranges.

# GATE 2023 CS: Q48

**Question:** [Official archived paper page 51](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2023/cs_2023.pdf#page=51); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** D

**Derived answer:** D

**Paraphrased problem context:** Let U={1,...,n}, n>1000, and disjoint A,B⊆U each have size k>0. Count permutations in which every A-element precedes every B-element or every B-element precedes every A-element; other elements may appear anywhere.

## Solution

Choose the 2k positions occupied by A∪B in binomial(n, 2k) ways. For A before B, the first k chosen positions receive A in k! orders and the last k receive B in k! orders. The remaining elements occupy other positions in(n−2k)! orders. The reversed block order contributes an equal, disjoint count because k>0. Total 2 binomial(n, 2k)(k!)²(n−2k)!, option D. Existence of the disjoint sets implicitly guarantees2k≤n.

Teaching detail. A and B need not occupy consecutive positions in the full permutation: unrelated U-elements may be interspersed. After choosing their 2k positions, requiring A before B completely determines which k positions are reserved for each set but leaves internal order free. As an alternative check, all binomial(2k,k) patterns of A/B labels among those positions are equally likely, and exactly two are favorable. Multiplying n! by 2/binomial(2k,k) gives the same expression. When k=1 every permutation qualifies, and the formula correctly reduces to n!.

# GATE 2023 CS: Q49

**Question:** [Official archived paper page 52](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2023/cs_2023.pdf#page=52); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** B, C, D

**Derived answer:** B;C;D

**Paraphrased problem context:** For surjection f:A→B on nonempty sets, define x~y iff f(x)=f(y), and let E be its equivalence classes. Define F:E→B by F([x])=f(x). Select true claims: A not well-defined; B surjective; C injective; D bijective.

## Solution

F is well-defined because all representatives of one class have the same f-value. It is onto because every b∈B has an f-preimage x and hence class[x]. If F([x])=F([y]), then f(x)=f(y), so x~y and[x]=[y], proving injectivity. Therefore F is bijective as well. Answer B, C, D.

Teaching detail. The equivalence classes are exactly the nonempty fibers f^−1({b}). Replacing x with another representative of its class cannot change f(x), which proves the mapping is legitimate before discussing injectivity. Surjectivity of the original f ensures that no element of B lacks a fiber. Distinct classes cannot map to the same b, because that would make their representatives equivalent and hence put them in the same class. The construction collapses the only source of noninjectivity of f, turning the induced mapping into a bijection.

# GATE 2023 CS: Q51

**Question:** [Official archived paper page 54](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2023/cs_2023.pdf#page=54); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** A, D

**Derived answer:** A, D

**Paraphrased problem context:** On the powerset 2^X, define A Δ B=(A−B)∪(B−A). Select true general statements: A (2^X,Δ) is a group; B inverses exist but it is not a group; C the inverse of A is its complement in X; D the inverse of A is A.

## Solution

Membership in a symmetric difference is exclusive-or of the two membership bits. Thus the operation is closed and associative, the identity is the empty set, and A Δ A is empty for every A. This proves the group property and makes every element its own inverse: A and D. B incorrectly denies the group property. A complement is not generally an inverse: A Δ (X minus A)=X, not the empty identity (except in the degenerate case X empty).

Teaching detail. For each element x∈X, membership in A Δ B is 1 exactly when the two membership bits differ. XOR is associative and commutative, so applying this pointwise establishes those properties for sets, not merely for a sample. The empty set contributes a zero bit everywhere and is the identity. A set XOR itself gives zero everywhere, proving the inverse claim and the group axioms. For nonempty X, taking A=∅ immediately refutes the complement-as-inverse claim: ∅ Δ X=X, not ∅.

# GATE 2023 CS: Q55

**Question:** [Official archived paper page 58](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2023/cs_2023.pdf#page=58); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** A, B

**Derived answer:** A, B

**Paraphrased problem context:** For a simple finite undirected graph, process vertices in a fixed order and assign each the smallest positive color absent from already colored neighbors. With maximum degree Δ, select valid guarantees: A proper coloring; B at most Δ+1 colors; C at most Δ colors; D exactly the chromatic number of colors.

## Solution

When a vertex is colored, its chosen color differs from every already-colored neighbor. Each edge therefore has distinct colors at its endpoints after its later endpoint is processed, proving A. At most degree(v) colors are forbidden, so one of the first degree(v)+1 colors is available, proving B. C fails on any complete graph, which needs Delta+1 colors. D fails for a path a-b-c-d processed in the order a, d, b, c: the colors are respectively 1, 1, 2, 3, although the graph is bipartite and has chromatic number 2.

Teaching detail. The proper-coloring invariant can be proved edge by edge: for every edge uv, whichever endpoint is processed later explicitly avoids the earlier endpoint's color. For the degree bound, a vertex with d neighbors sees at most d distinct forbidden colors, so among colors 1 through d+1 at least one remains. On a complete graph with r vertices, Δ=r−1 but r colors are necessary, refuting C. The four-vertex path example refutes optimality without compromising correctness: greedy remains proper while using an avoidable third color.
