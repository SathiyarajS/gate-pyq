**Paraphrased problem context:** A set S has n elements. How many ordered pairs belong to the largest and smallest equivalence relations on S, respectively?
Options:
(A) n and n
(B) n² and n
(C) n² and 0
(D) n and 1

## Solution

1. Approach. An equivalence relation must be reflexive, symmetric and transitive. Test the two extremes: all possible pairs, and only the n self-pairs.

2. Derivation and interpretation. The universal relation S×S is an equivalence relation with n² pairs. Reflexivity requires every diagonal pair (s,s), and those n pairs alone form the identity equivalence relation. Hence the largest and smallest counts are n² and n, B.

# GATE 2007: Q4 (printed CS Q4)

**Question:** [Official-source archive](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1popAHO-cX69oQEx_tD0wZXL1tTrBljeO/view), member CS/CS2007.pdf, physical page 2. Consult original wording and choices.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** B for the minimal nonplanar core

**Paraphrased problem context:** Let G be a nonplanar graph with the minimum possible number of edges. Which size is intended for G?
Options:
(A) 9 edges, 5 vertices
(B) 9 edges, 6 vertices
(C) 10 edges, 5 vertices
(D) 10 edges, 6 vertices

## Solution

1. Approach. Compare the two minimal nonplanar cores K5 and K3,3. Subdividing edges cannot reduce their edge counts. Be careful that isolated vertices do not affect planarity or edge count.

2. Derivation and interpretation. By Kuratowski’s theorem a nonplanar graph contains a subdivision of K5 or K3,3. Such a subgraph needs at least 10 or 9 edges respectively. K3,3 attains the 9-edge minimum with 6 vertices, giving B. The vertex count is not forced by edge minimality alone: isolated vertices can be added without changing the 9 edges or nonplanarity. Thus 6 is the minimum/core vertex count, not a universal claim for every graph minimizing only edges.

**Paraphrased problem context:** How many pairwise nonisomorphic Abelian groups of order 4 exist?
Options:
(A) 2
(B) 3
(C) 4
(D) 5

## Solution

1. Approach. Lagrange’s theorem permits element orders 1, 2 or 4. Divide the classification into whether an element of order 4 exists; the remaining case forces all three nonidentity elements to square to the identity.

2. Derivation and interpretation. An element of order 4 generates cyclic C4. If none exists, every nonidentity element has order 2, giving C2×C2, the Klein four-group. These are nonisomorphic because their element orders differ and exhaust the possibilities. Thus 2, A.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Graph(x) means x is a graph and Connected(x) means x is connected. Which formula does NOT express “Not every graph is connected”?
Options:
(A) ¬∀x(Graph(x)⇒Connected(x))
(B) ∃x(Graph(x)∧¬Connected(x))
(C) ¬∀x(¬Graph(x)∨Connected(x))
(D) ∀x(Graph(x)⇒¬Connected(x))

## Solution

1. Approach. Negate “every graph is connected” by moving the negation through the universal quantifier, then through the implication. The result asserts at least one disconnected graph, not that all graphs are disconnected.

2. Derivation and interpretation. Not every graph is connected means ∃x(Graph(x)∧¬Connected(x)), B. Negating the universal implication gives A, and rewriting implication as ¬Graph∨Connected gives C. D instead says every graph is disconnected, a stronger and different claim. Hence D.

**Independently derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** Which graph is guaranteed to have an Eulerian circuit?
Options:
(A) Any k-regular graph with even k
(B) The complete graph on 90 vertices
(C) The complement of a cycle on 25 vertices
(D) None of these

## Solution

1. Approach. For a nontrivial undirected graph, all nonisolated vertices must be connected and have even degrees. Check connectedness as well as parity; even degrees alone do not suffice.

2. Derivation and interpretation. The complement of C25 has degree 24−2=22 at each vertex and is connected, so it has an Euler circuit, C. An even-regular graph need not be connected, so A is not guaranteed. K90 has odd degree 89 and is not Eulerian. The connectedness of the complement follows because any two nonadjacent vertices were neighbors in the cycle and share a vertex nonadjacent to both on that cycle.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Choose uniformly from all 20! permutations of 1,2,…,20. What is the probability that 2 appears before every other even number?
Options:
(A) 1/2
(B) 1/10
(C) 9!/20!
(D) None of these

## Solution

1. Approach. Ignore the odd numbers and examine only the relative order of the ten even numbers. Symmetry gives each even number the same chance of occupying the first even position.

2. Derivation and interpretation. There are ten even numbers among 1,...,20. Their relative order is uniformly random, and each is equally likely to be first among them. Therefore the chance that 2 is earliest is 1/10, B.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Let S={a,b,c,d}. The partitions are π1={{a,b,c,d}}, π2={{a,b},{c,d}}, π3={{a,b,c},{d}}, π4={{a},{b},{c},{d}}. Define πi≺πj when πi refines πj. Which Hasse diagram represents these four partitions? Options are described by bottom-to-top cover relations.
Options:
(A) Chain π4<π3<π2<π1
(B) Diamond with bottom π3, middle π2 and π4, top π1
(C) Diamond with bottom π4, middle π2 and π3, top π1
(D) π4 below each of π1,π2,π3, which are pairwise incomparable

## Solution

1. Approach. A refinement splits blocks but never joins elements from different old blocks. Check whether every block of one partition is contained in a block of the other.

2. Derivation and interpretation. The all-singleton partition π4 refines both π2={ab,cd} and π3={abc,d}; both refine the one-block π1={abcd}. π2 and π3 are incomparable: cd crosses π3’s blocks, while abc crosses π2’s blocks. The Hasse diagram is the diamond with π4 at bottom and π1 at top, C.

# GATE 2007: Q48 (printed CS Q48)

**Question:** [Official-source archive](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1popAHO-cX69oQEx_tD0wZXL1tTrBljeO/view), member CS/CS2007.pdf, physical page 11. Consult original wording and choices.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** A

**Paraphrased problem context:** Which claim is true for formulas in conjunctive normal form under the usual nonempty-clause convention?
Options:
(A) Every formula has an assignment satisfying at least half its clauses
(B) Every formula has an assignment satisfying all its clauses
(C) Some formula has at most one quarter of its clauses satisfied under every assignment
(D) None of these

## Solution

1. Approach. Pair any truth assignment with its complement. Each nonempty disjunctive clause has some literal, and that literal is true in exactly one of the two assignments.

2. Derivation and interpretation. For the conventional setting in which each clause contains at least one literal, consider any assignment and its bitwise complement. Every clause is satisfied by at least one of the two: choose any literal in it, which is true in one assignment. Hence the two assignments together satisfy at least m clause occurrences, and one satisfies at least m/2. This proves A. B fails for (x)∧(¬x), and the same half-clause guarantee rules out C. If empty clauses are permitted, the unstated nonempty-clause assumption matters: a formula consisting only of empty clauses is never satisfied at all, so A would fail and C could hold. The intended CNF convention excludes this degenerate case.

**Paraphrased problem context:** A robot starts at (0,0). Each move increases either its x-coordinate or y-coordinate by one, so from (i,j) it can move to (i+1,j) or (i,j+1). How many paths reach (10,10)? C(n,k) denotes a binomial coefficient.
Options:
(A) C(20,10)
(B) 2^20
(C) 2^10
(D) None of these

## Solution

1. Approach. Every valid path contains exactly ten right moves and ten up moves. Specify which ten of the twenty move positions are right moves; the rest are then fixed.

2. Derivation and interpretation. A path from (0,0) to (10,10) requires exactly ten right and ten up moves. Choose the positions of the ten right moves among the twenty total moves. The count is C(20,10)=184756, option A.

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** D

**Paraphrased problem context:** A robot moves one unit right or up at each step, starting at (0,0) and ending at (10,10). It may not traverse the edge (4,4)→(5,4). How many paths remain? C(n,k) denotes a binomial coefficient.
Options:
(A) 2^9
(B) 2^19
(C) C(8,4)×C(11,5)
(D) C(20,10)−C(8,4)×C(11,5)

## Solution

1. Approach. Count forbidden paths by splitting at the forbidden edge. Choose a path to (4,4), traverse the single forbidden step, then choose a path from (5,4) to the destination. Subtract this product from all paths.

2. Derivation and interpretation. Subtract paths using the forbidden edge (4,4)->(5,4) from all C(20,10) paths. A path using that edge has C(8,4) choices to reach (4,4), then the fixed forbidden step, then C(11,5) choices for five right and six up moves from (5,4) to (10,10). Thus the permitted count is C(20,10)-C(8,4)*C(11,5)=184756-70*462=152416, option D.
