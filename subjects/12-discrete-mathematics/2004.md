**Paraphrased problem context:** Translate “Some boys in the class are taller than all the girls.” The domain is the class and taller(x,y) means x is taller than y. Options below follow GFG; GateForum prints ∧ instead of → inside option D, making its B and D identical and neither a correct translation.
Options:
(A) ∃x[boy(x)→∀y(girl(y)∧taller(x,y))]
(B) ∃x[boy(x)∧∀y(girl(y)∧taller(x,y))]
(C) ∃x[boy(x)→∀y(girl(y)→taller(x,y))]

## Solution

2. Teaching check: An existential implication could be satisfied merely by choosing a person who is not a boy. A universal conjunction would require everyone in the domain to be a girl. The outer conjunction and inner implication prevent these two distinct logical errors. The answer D is source-qualified to GFG’s correctly printed option.

**Paraphrased problem context:** On the nonnegative integers, S={(x,y):y=x+1}. Find its reflexive transitive closure. GFG prints the domain {0,1,2,…}; GateForum omits the ellipsis and prints {0,1,2}, for which the same order condition applies restricted to that finite domain.
Options:
(A) {(x,y):y>x}
(B) {(x,y):y≥x}
(C) {(x,y):y<x}
(D) {(x,y):y≤x}

## Solution

1. A path of k successor steps takes x to x+k, for any integer k≥0. Zero steps supply reflexivity.

2. Thus the closure is{(x,y):y≥x, x,y nonnegative integers}, B.

3. Teaching check: Reflexive closure adds (x,x); transitivity adds (x,x+2),(x,x+3), and all later successors. No operation can create a pair with y<x because every original step increases the value. On the Forum finite domain, the resulting pairs are (0,0),(0,1),(0,2),(1,1),(1,2),(2,2).

2. Hence A. The same two assignments establish the same classification for the GateForum variant with (P∧Q)→R as consequent.

3. Teaching check: To show satisfiable but not valid, one true assignment and one false assignment suffice. With R=true, the consequent is true. With P=Q=true,R=false, the antecedent is true and the consequent false. Both witnesses also work for the alternate ∧ formula.

Options:
(A) c,a,e,b
(B) c,b,a,e
(C) c,b,e,a
(D) c,e,a,b

## Solution

2. The c row under columns e,a,b,c is a³,a⁴,a⁵,a⁶, namely c,e,a,b. This is D.

3. Teaching check: Associativity lets powers of a determine the entire subgroup generated by a. Since e,a,a²=b,a³=c are four distinct elements, this is the whole group. Multiplying c=a³ by e,a,a²,a³ reduces exponents modulo four and yields c,e,a,b.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Let S={{1,2},{1,2,3},{1,3,5},{1,2,4},{1,2,3,4,5}}, ordered by containment. Which listed minimal collection of sets must be adjoined to make a complete lattice? “Necessary” is interpreted as the minimal sufficient offered augmentation, not a claim excluding every other possible bottom element.
Options:
(A) {1}
(B) {1} and {2,3}
(C) {1} and {1,3}
(D) {1}, {1,3}, {1,2,3,4}, {1,2,3,5}

## Solution

1. Write U={1,2}, V={1,2,3}, W={1,3,5}, X={1,2,4}, and T={1,2,3,4,5}. Adjoin B={1}.

2. Comparable pairs already have their meet and join. The incomparable pairs (U,W), (V,W), and (X,W) have meet B and join T; (V,X) has meet U and join T.

3. Thus every pair has meet/join, and the finite nonempty poset with bottom B and top T is complete. Adding only {1}, option A, is sufficient and is the minimal augmentation among the offered choices.

4. Teaching check: A lattice’s join must be the least upper bound within the displayed poset, not necessarily the ordinary union set if that set is absent. Similarly the meet is the greatest available lower bound. After adding {1}, a finite bounded lattice is complete, including empty-family meet and join.

**Paraphrased problem context:** An exam has 150 one-mark multiple-choice questions, each with four choices. Correct answers earn 1; incorrect answers earn −0.25. If 1000 students choose all answers uniformly at random, what is the sum of their expected marks?
Options:
(A) 0
(B) 2550

## Solution

3. Teaching check: The negative mark is one quarter, not one third. Therefore random guessing has positive expectation here: (1/4)×1+(3/4)×(−1/4)=1/16 per question. Zero expectation would require a different wrong-answer penalty.

**Paraphrased problem context:** All nonzero entries of an M×N matrix are covered by the union of a specified a rows and b columns. What universal upper bound holds on the number of nonzero entries that can be selected with no two in the same row or column?
Options:
(A) ≤a+b
(B) ≤max(a,b)
(C) ≤min(M−a,N−b)
(D) ≤min(a,b)

## Solution

1. Treat each nonzero entry as an edge from its row vertex to its column vertex. The stated a rows together with b columns cover all such edges.

2. Any selection with no two entries in the same row or column is a matching. Each chosen entry must use at least one of the a+b covering vertices, and no covering vertex can serve two chosen entries.

3. Consequently the selection has at most a+b entries, A. The bound can be attained with edges using distinct other endpoints; it need not equal the actual maximum.

4. Teaching check: The covering rows and columns form a vertex cover in a bipartite graph. A matching cannot reuse any covering vertex, so its size is at most the cover size. This does not require invoking the stronger theorem equating maximum matching and minimum cover.

6. Teaching check: The argument needs both a lower and an upper bound: forcing a contradiction under three colours proves at least four; the explicit assignment proves at most four. Merely observing a triangle would establish only a lower bound of three.

**Paraphrased problem context:** G1=(V,E1) and G2=(V,E2) are connected graphs on the same vertex set with more than two vertices. Their intersection (V,E1∩E2) is disconnected. What must be true of their union (V,E1∪E2)?
Options:
(A) It cannot have a cut vertex
(B) It must have a cycle
(C) It must have a bridge
(D) Its chromatic number exceeds those of both G1 and G2

## Solution

1. The union is connected because it contains connected G1. If the union had no cycle, it would be a tree.

2. Every connected spanning subgraph of a tree must contain all tree edges: deleting any edge disconnects it. Thus both G1 and G2 would equal that tree, making their intersection connected, a contradiction.

3. Therefore the union must contain a cycle, B. This does not force a bridge or eliminate articulation vertices, and a cycle need not increase chromatic number over both constituent graphs.

4. Teaching check: A connected acyclic union would be a tree, and every tree edge is indispensable to every connected spanning subgraph. Hence both input graphs would contain all its edges, contradicting the disconnected intersection. This contradiction proves a cycle must exist.
