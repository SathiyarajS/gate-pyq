**Paraphrased problem context:** Let p="It is raining", q="It is cold", r="It is pleasant". Translate "It is not raining and it is pleasant, and it is not pleasant only if it is raining and it is cold." A:(¬p∧r)∧(¬r→(p∧q)). B:(¬p∧r)∧((p∧q)→¬r). C:(¬p∧r)∨((p∧q)→¬r). D:(¬p∧r)∨(r→(p∧q)).

## Solution

1. The first clause is ¬p∧r. The phrase A only if B translates as A→B, since B is a necessary condition for A. Thus the second clause is ¬r→(p∧q). Joining clauses with and gives option A directly.

2. There is a semantic nonuniqueness in the printed alternatives. In either A or B, a true first conjunct forces p=false and r=true. Then A's implication has false antecedent ¬r, while B's implication has false antecedent p∧q. Both are true regardless of q.

3. If the first conjunct is false, both full conjunctions are false. Consequently both A and B are logically equivalent to ¬p∧r for every valuation. Preserve official answer A as the direct structural translation, but do not claim that B has a different truth table. C and D incorrectly use a disjunction between the clauses.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Let X={a,b,c,d,e}. The partial order includes reflexive pairs and (a,b),(a,c),(a,d),(a,e),(b,c),(b,e),(c,e),(d,e). Its Hasse diagram has two upward chains a<b<c<e and a<d<e. What minimum number of ordered pairs must be added to make it a lattice?

## Solution

1. A lattice requires a unique greatest lower bound (meet) and least upper bound (join) for every pair of elements. A pair of comparable elements already has these: the smaller is their meet and the larger their join.

2. In this diagram the only incomparable unordered pairs are {b,d} and {c,d}. For either pair, the only common lower bound is a and the only common upper bound is e. Hence their meet is a and their join is e.

3. All pairs therefore already have the required bounds, so no ordered pair needs adding: answer 0. This is the pentagon lattice, which need not be distributive; being nondistributive does not prevent it from being a lattice.

**Paraphrased problem context:** An undirected graph G has n vertices and 25 edges, and every vertex has degree at least 3. What is the maximum possible n?

## Solution

1. By the handshaking lemma, the sum of all vertex degrees is twice the edge count, 50. Since each of n vertices contributes at least 3, we have 3n≤50. Therefore n≤floor(50/3)=16.

2. A numerical upper bound is not enough; show that 16 is attainable. Start with a cycle on 16 vertices, providing 16 edges and degree 2 at each vertex. Add the 8 edges joining opposite vertices around the cycle. This gives a simple 3-regular graph with 24 edges.

3. Add any further edge not already present. It raises two degrees to 4 and leaves all others at 3, with exactly 25 edges and 16 vertices. Thus the maximum is indeed 16.

**Paraphrased problem context:** The ordinary generating function of sequence (a_n) for n≥0 is (1+z)/(1−z)³. Find a3−a0.

## Solution

1. Use the binomial-series identity (1-z)^-3=Σ(n≥0)C(n+2,2)z^n. Its first coefficients are 1,3,6,10,… .

2. Multiplication by 1+z adds each coefficient to the preceding one. Thus a0=1 and a3=C(5,2)+C(4,2)=10+6=16. Directly, (1+z)(1+3z+6z²+10z³+…)=1+4z+9z²+16z³+… .

3. Therefore a3-a0=16-1=15. More generally a_n=C(n+2,2)+C(n+1,2)=(n+1)² for n≥1. The numerator shifts coefficients; it cannot simply be ignored when reading a3.

**Paraphrased problem context:** X has a Poisson distribution with mean 5. Find E[(X+2)²].

## Solution

1. A Poisson variable has variance equal to its mean, so E[X]=5 and Var(X)=5. The second-moment identity Var(X)=E[X²]-(E[X])² gives E[X²]=5+25=30.

2. Expand before taking expectations: (X+2)²=X²+4X+4. Linearity of expectation therefore gives E[(X+2)²]=30+4·5+4=54.

3. A useful check is Var(X+2)+(E[X+2])²=5+7²=54, since adding a constant does not change variance. Squaring the mean alone would give 49 and omit the variance contribution.

**Paraphrased problem context:** T is a tree with 10 vertices. Find the sum of degrees of all vertices.

## Solution

1. A finite undirected tree is connected and acyclic. Its edge count is number of vertices minus one, so this tree has 10−1=9 edges.

2. Sum the degrees by counting edge ends: every edge contributes exactly one to each of its two endpoints. Hence Σdeg(v)=2|E|.

3. The result is 2×9=18. This does not depend on whether the tree is a path, star, or another shape.

**Paraphrased problem context:** The source says (p→q)→r is “a contradiction.” Classify (r→p)→q: A tautology; B contradiction; C always true when p is false; D always true when q is true. Distinguish the source’s literal logical terminology from its intended false-valuation reading.

## Solution

1. Under the intended interpretation, an implication A→r is false exactly when A is true and r is false. Therefore p→q is true and r=false.

2. With r=false, r→p is true regardless of p. Hence (r→p)→q reduces to true→q, namely q. It is true whenever q is true, D.

3. The allowed valuation p=false,q=false,r=false makes the target false, ruling out both tautology and C. Taking q=true makes it true, ruling out contradiction. Literally, the first expression is not a contradiction over independent atoms: setting r=true satisfies it.
