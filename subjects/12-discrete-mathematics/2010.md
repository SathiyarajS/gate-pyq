

# GATE 2010: Q11 (printed CS Q1)

**Question:** [Official-source archive](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1popAHO-cX69oQEx_tD0wZXL1tTrBljeO/view), member CS/CS2010.pdf, physical page 2. Consult original wording and choices.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** For a graph G=(V,E), define ξ(G)=Σ_d i_d·d, where i_d is the number of vertices of degree d. S and T are two different trees satisfying ξ(S)=ξ(T). Which relation holds between their numbers of vertices |S| and |T|?
Options: (A) |S|=2|T|; (B) |S|=|T|−1; (C) |S|=|T|; (D) |S|=|T|+1.

## Solution

The defined sum counts every vertex degree and hence equals 2|E| by the handshake lemma. A tree with v vertices has v−1 edges, so ξ=2(v−1). Equal ξ for S and T implies equal vertex counts |S|=|T|, C; the trees need not be isomorphic.

Teaching detail: Why the degree sum works: each edge has two endpoints, so contributes exactly two to Σ_d i_d d. Writing s=|S| and t=|T| gives 2(s−1)=2(t−1), hence s=t. “Different” need not mean different sizes: two nonisomorphic trees can have the same vertex count and therefore the same degree sum.

**Independently derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** How many reflexive relations exist on a set with five elements?
Options: (A) 2^10; (B) 2^15; (C) 2^20; (D) 2^25.

## Solution

A relation on five elements is a subset of 25 ordered pairs. Reflexivity forces the five diagonal pairs to be present. Each of the other 20 pairs can independently be present or absent, giving 2²⁰ relations, C.

Teaching detail: Label the elements 1,…,5 and draw a 5×5 Boolean relation matrix. All diagonal entries must be 1; the 20 off-diagonal entries are free binary choices. The answer is not 2^25 because that counts all relations, including nonreflexive ones. Reflexivity does not impose symmetry, so (i,j) and (j,i) remain independent choices.

# GATE 2010: Q14 (printed CS Q4)

**Question:** [Official-source archive](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1popAHO-cX69oQEx_tD0wZXL1tTrBljeO/view), member CS/CS2010.pdf, physical page 2. Consult original wording and choices.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** A

**Paraphrased problem context:** Let S={1,ω,ω²}, where ω and ω² are the nonreal cube roots of unity. With * denoting multiplication, which algebraic structure does (S,*) form?
Options: (A) A group; (B) A ring; (C) An integral domain; (D) A field.

## Solution

The set {1,ω,ω²} is closed under multiplication because exponents reduce modulo 3. Multiplication is associative, 1 is the identity, and ω and ω² are inverses of each other. Hence it forms a cyclic group of order 3, A. A ring, integral domain or field additionally requires an additive operation and its associated axioms, not just this multiplicative structure.

Teaching detail: A generator is ω: its powers are 1,ω,ω²,ω³=1. The multiplication table therefore cycles through these three elements. In particular, ω·ω²=1 and (ω²)²=ω. A field would require closure under an addition making an abelian group, including an additive identity; the specified single operation supplies no such structure.

**Question:** [Official-source archive](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1popAHO-cX69oQEx_tD0wZXL1tTrBljeO/view), member CS/CS2010.pdf, physical page 6. Consult original wording and choices.

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** A

**Paraphrased problem context:** A computer assembly is faulty with probability p. A test gives the correct result for any computer with probability q. What is the probability that a computer is declared faulty?
Options: (A) pq+(1−p)(1−q); (B) (1−q)p; (C) (1−p)q; (D) pq.

## Solution

Partition computers into faulty with probability p and nonfaulty with probability 1−p. The test declares a faulty computer faulty with conditional probability q, while it incorrectly declares a nonfaulty computer faulty with probability 1−q. Total probability gives pq+(1−p)(1−q), option A. The phrase correct result for any computer supplies the same conditional accuracy q in each class.

Teaching detail: A probability tree has two paths ending in “declared faulty”: truly faulty then correctly identified, probability pq; or truly sound then incorrectly classified, probability (1−p)(1−q). These events are disjoint, so add them. Checks: if q=1 the result is p; if q=0 the result is 1−p; if q=1/2 it is 1/2 regardless of p.

# GATE 2010: Q37 (printed CS Q27)

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** A

**Paraphrased problem context:** A positive divisor of 10^99 is selected uniformly at random. What is the probability that it is a multiple of 10^96?
Options: (A) 1/625; (B) 4/625; (C) 12/625; (D) 16/625.

## Solution

Since 10⁹⁹=2⁹⁹5⁹⁹, every positive divisor is uniquely 2^a5^b with 0≤a,b≤99, giving 100² equally likely divisors. Being a multiple of 10⁹⁶ requires a,b≥96, leaving four choices for each exponent. Probability=16/10000=1/625, option A. Uniform choice is over divisors, not over integers up to 10⁹⁹.

Teaching detail: The admissible exponents for each prime are 96,97,98,99, so the favorable divisors number 4×4=16. Divisibility requires both prime exponents to be large enough; satisfying only the exponent of 2 or only the exponent of 5 is insufficient. Reducing 16/10000 gives the offered 1/625.

# GATE 2010: Q38 (printed CS Q28)

**Question:** [Official-source archive](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1popAHO-cX69oQEx_tD0wZXL1tTrBljeO/view), member CS/CS2010.pdf, physical page 6. Consult original wording and choices.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** D

**Paraphrased problem context:** Which sequences cannot be degree sequences of any simple graph? I: (7,6,5,4,4,3,2,1); II: (6,6,6,6,3,3,2,2); III: (7,6,6,4,4,3,2,2); IV: (8,7,7,6,4,2,1,1).
Options: (A) I and II; (B) III and IV; (C) IV only; (D) II and IV.

## Solution

IV has eight vertices but a degree of 8, impossible in a simple graph. For II, the four highest degrees sum to 24; their vertices can contribute at most 4·3=12 internally and at most 3+3+2+2=10 from the other vertices, totaling only 22. Thus II also fails. Havel–Hakimi reduces I via (5,4,3,3,2,1,0) to (3,2,2,1,0,0), then (1,1,0,0,0), then zeros. III reduces via (5,5,3,3,2,1,1) to (4,2,2,1,1,0), then (1,1,0,0,0), then zeros. Hence I and III are graphical and exactly II and IV fail: D.

Teaching detail: The even degree-sum condition alone cannot settle this question: all four sums are even. Sequence II fails an Erdős–Gallai bound on its four degree-six vertices: a vertex inside that set has only three possible neighbors inside it, while vertices outside cannot supply more incidences than their own degrees. Sequence IV already violates the individual maximum n−1=7.

# GATE 2010: Q40 (printed CS Q30)

**Question:** [Official-source archive](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1popAHO-cX69oQEx_tD0wZXL1tTrBljeO/view), member CS/CS2010.pdf, physical page 6. Consult original wording and choices.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** Not available; no official key located.

**Independently derived answer:** B

**Paraphrased problem context:** F(x,y,t) means person x can fool person y at time t. Which sentence best expresses ∀x∃y∃t ¬F(x,y,t)?
Options: (A) Everyone can fool some person at some time; (B) No one can fool everyone all the time; (C) Everyone cannot fool some person all the time; (D) No one can fool some person at some time.

## Solution

The formula ∀x∃y∃t ¬F(x,y,t) says that for each person x there is at least one person y and time t when x cannot fool y. Equivalently, ∀x ¬∀y∀t F(x,y,t): no one can fool everyone all the time. That is B. It does not prohibit fooling some people at some times.

Teaching detail: Negating “x can fool everyone all the time” moves the negation across two universal quantifiers: ¬∀y∀t F becomes ∃y∃t ¬F. The outer ∀x then says this failure occurs for every would-be deceiver. The witnesses y and t may depend on x; the formula does not assert a single universally unfoolable person or a single common time.
