

# GATE 2021 CS1: Q17 (printed CS Q7)

**Question:** [Official archived paper page 15](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2021/cs_2021.pdf#page=15); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** B

**Derived answer:** B

**Paraphrased problem context:** For propositions p,q, let S1=(¬p∧(p∨q))→q and S2=q→(¬p∧(p∨q)). Which are tautologies? A both; B S1 only; C S2 only; D neither.

## Solution

The antecedent ¬p∧(p∨q) simplifies to ¬p∧q. Therefore S1 is (¬p∧q)→q, a tautology. S2 is q→(¬p∧q), which is false when p=q=true. Hence only S1 is a tautology, B.

Distributivity gives ¬p∧(p∨q)=(¬p∧p)∨(¬p∧q)=¬p∧q. Any valuation making S1's antecedent true already makes q true, so it cannot be a false implication. To refute S2 it suffices to exhibit one valuation: p=true,q=true makes antecedent true but consequent false. Tautology requires all valuations, not just some.

**Official answer:** 11 to 11

**Derived answer:** 11

**Paraphrased problem context:** A connected undirected planar graph has eight vertices and five faces. How many edges does it have? Count the unbounded face among the five.

## Solution

For a connected planar embedding, Euler’s formula is V−E+F=2, including the unbounded face. Hence E=8+5−2=11.

Rearrange Euler's formula as E=V+F−2 and substitute the given values. A tree would have E=V−1 and F=1, illustrating that the exterior region is a face. Here five faces imply four independent cycles, also checked by E−V+1=11−8+1=4. Omitting the exterior face would shift the answer by one.

**Derived answer:** 65

**Paraphrased problem context:** Assign six distinct jobs of distinct difficulty to three distinct computers of distinct speeds. The fastest must get the toughest job, the slowest the easiest, and each computer must receive at least one job. How many assignments are possible?

## Solution

Fix the toughest job on the fastest computer and the easiest on the slowest. Each of the four remaining distinct jobs has 3 destination choices, giving3⁴. Subtract assignments that leave the middle-speed computer empty: all four then go to the other two computers, giving2⁴. Thus3⁴−2⁴=81−16=65.

The extreme jobs are fixed, leaving four labeled jobs, not four indistinguishable units. The fastest and slowest computers are already nonempty, so only the middle computer requires an inclusion-exclusion correction. For a second count, assign exactly k of the four remaining jobs to the middle computer: Σ_(k=1)^4 binom(4,k)2^(4−k)=32+24+8+1=65.

# GATE 2021 CS1: Q44 (printed CS Q34)

**Question:** [Official archived paper page 29](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2021/cs_2021.pdf#page=29); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** B

**Derived answer:** B

**Paraphrased problem context:** G is a group of order 6 and H is a subgroup with 1<|H|<6. Which is correct? A both always cyclic; B G may not be cyclic but H always is; C G always cyclic but H may not be; D neither need be cyclic.

## Solution

Lagrange’s theorem makes the nontrivial proper subgroup order either2 or3. A group of prime order is cyclic, so H is always cyclic. G need not be cyclic: the permutation group S3 has order6 and is nonabelian. Thus B.

Lagrange's theorem says |H| divides |G|=6, so the proper nontrivial choices are exactly 2 and 3. In a group of prime order p, any nonidentity element has order dividing p and greater than 1, hence order p; its powers generate the whole group. To disprove cyclicity of G it is enough to give one counterexample, S3. A cyclic group of order 6 also exists, so 'may not' is the appropriate wording rather than 'never'.

# GATE 2021 CS1: Q46 (printed CS Q36)

**Question:** [Official archived paper page 30](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2021/cs_2021.pdf#page=30); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** A

**Derived answer:** A

**Paraphrased problem context:** G is an undirected unweighted connected graph with adjacency matrix M and diameter equal to its largest shortest-path distance. Let P=M² and define G2 on the same vertices by N_ij=1 if M_ij>0 or P_ij>0, otherwise 0. Which holds universally? A diam(G2)≤⌈diam(G)/2⌉; B ⌈diam(G)/2⌉<diam(G2)<diam(G); C diam(G2)=diam(G); D diam(G)<diam(G2)≤2 diam(G).

## Solution

G2 connects vertices whose original distance is at most2. A shortest G path of lengthd can be compressed into ceil(d/2) G2 edges. Conversely each G2 edge expands into at most2 G edges, so no shorter G2 path than ceil(d/2) is possible. Thus d_G2(u, v)=ceil(d_G(u, v)/2), and diam(G2)=ceil(diam(G)/2), which in particular satisfies A’s ≤ bound. Self-loops from M² do not shorten distances.

Each positive entry of M² represents a two-edge walk, so a G2 edge can cover at most two original edges. A d-edge shortest path can be grouped into pairs, with one singleton if d is odd, giving an upper bound ⌈d/2⌉. Expanding any k-edge G2 path yields a G walk of at most 2k edges, proving k≥⌈d/2⌉. Taking the maximum over all vertex pairs establishes equality, which is stronger than the selected non-strict bound.

# GATE 2021 CS1: Q53 (printed CS Q43)

**Question:** [Official archived paper page 35](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2021/cs_2021.pdf#page=35); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** C

**Derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** A relation R is circular when aRb and bRc imply cRa. Select the sufficient conditions for a relation S to be an equivalence relation: A reflexive and symmetric; B circular and symmetric; C reflexive and circular; D transitive and circular.

## Solution

If S is reflexive and circular, aSb together with bSb implies bSa, establishing symmetry. If aSb and bSc, circularity gives cSa, and symmetry then gives aSc; hence S is transitive as well, proving C. Reflexive and symmetric need not imply transitive: take all loops on {1, 2, 3}, pairs (1, 2),(2, 1),(2, 3),(3, 2), but omit (1, 3). This refutes A. The empty relation on a nonempty set is circular, symmetric and transitive but not reflexive, refuting B and D.

An equivalence relation must satisfy three independent obligations: reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity. Under C, reflexivity supplies bSb to combine with aSb and derive bSa by circularity. Once symmetry is established, aSb and bSc give cSa by circularity and then aSc by symmetry. The empty relation on any nonempty set satisfies every implication whose premise requires an existing pair, so it is circular, symmetric and transitive vacuously, yet it fails all reflexive loops. This explains both B and D counterexamples.

# GATE 2021 CS2: Q21 (printed CS Q11)

**Question:** [Official archived paper page 61](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2021/cs_2021.pdf#page=61); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** B; C

**Derived answer:** B; C

**Paraphrased problem context:** For n≥2, S1 is all n×n matrices over {a,b,c}; S2 is all functions {0,1,…,n²−1}→{0,1,2}. Select: A no bijection S1→S2; B a surjection exists; C a bijection exists; D no injection exists.

## Solution

There are3^(n²) matrices because each of n² entries has 3 choices. There are also3^(n²) functions from an n²-element domain to a 3-element codomain. A direct bijection reads matrix entries in a fixed order and maps a, b, c to 0, 1, 2. A bijection is also a surjection and an injection, so B, C.

Explicitly index matrix position (i,j), for zero-based i,j, by i*n+j. Map its symbol a,b,c to the function value 0,1,2. The inverse reconstructs each entry from that value, proving both injectivity and surjectivity without relying only on equal counts. A and D negate properties that this construction supplies.

**Paraphrased problem context:** S=((P∧Q)→R)→((P∧Q)→(Q→R)). Select A neither tautology nor contradiction; B tautology; C contradiction; D antecedent and consequent are equivalent.

## Solution

The antecedent simplifies to ¬P∨¬Q∨R. The consequent simplifies to ¬P∨¬Q∨(¬Q∨R), which is identical. Therefore S has the form E→E and is a tautology. Both B and D hold.

Using A→B≡¬A∨B, let E=¬P∨¬Q∨R. The outer statement is E→E=¬E∨E, true for all eight assignments of P,Q,R. Equivalence of antecedent and consequent establishes D separately from the overall tautology B; those are two selectable propositions, not competing single-choice answers.

**Official answer:** 59049 to 59049

**Derived answer:** 59049

**Paraphrased problem context:** S has ten elements. Count ordered pairs (A,B) with A⊆B⊆S. Equality is permitted.

## Solution

For each of the 10 elements choose exactly one of three states: outside B, in B but not A, or in A (and therefore in B). Choices are independent, giving3^10=59049 ordered pairs. The source symbol is subset-or-equal, so A=B is included.

A second derivation sums by |B|: for each k choose B in binomial(10,k) ways, then any of its 2^k subsets A. Thus Σ binomial(10,k)2^k=(1+2)^10. Counting 4^10 would allow the forbidden state “in A but outside B”; subtracting equality would answer the strict-subset variant rather than this question.
