**Paraphrased problem context:** Two fair coins are flipped. Given that at least one outcome is a head, what is the probability that both outcomes are heads?

(A) 1/3
(B) 1/4
(C) 1/2
(D) 2/3

## Solution

Condition on the event at least one head. For independent fair coins the remaining equally likely outcomes are HH, HT, TH. Exactly one has two heads, so the conditional probability is 1/3, A. This is event conditioning, not a different reporting protocol that selects one coin to reveal.

Before conditioning, HH, HT, TH and TT each have probability 1/4. The given event removes TT, leaving event probability 3/4. The desired intersection is simply HH, of probability 1/4. Hence P(HH given at least one H)=(1/4)/(3/4). Do not collapse HT and TH into one equally weighted case: they are two different outcomes and together have twice the probability of HH.

**Paraphrased problem context:** K4 is the complete graph on four vertices (the source draws a square with both diagonals). Q3 is the eight-vertex cube graph, with vertices the binary triples and an edge when triples differ in one bit. Which statement is TRUE?

(A) K4 is planar but Q3 is not
(B) Both K4 and Q3 are planar
(C) Q3 is planar while K3 is not (the source prints K3 here)
(D) Neither K4 nor Q3 is planar

## Solution

K4 has a planar embedding as a triangle with the fourth vertex inside joined to all three corners. Q3 has an embedding as nested squares joined at corresponding corners. Crossings in one drawing do not imply nonplanarity. Both graphs are planar, B.

Planarity is the existence of some drawing without edge crossings, not a property of one particular drawing. For K4, keep three vertices on an outer triangle and place the fourth inside; its three interior spokes do not cross. For Q3, draw one square inside another and join corresponding corners without crossings. These are explicit crossing-free embeddings. The source's K3 typo in C does not alter the correct B: K3 itself is also planar.

**Paraphrased problem context:** For a random variable with finite second moment, write R=E[X²]-(E[X])². Which is always true?

(A) R=0
(B) R<0
(C) R≥0
(D) R>0

## Solution

R=E[X²]−(E[X])²=E[(X−E[X])²]≥0 whenever the moments exist. A constant random variable attains0, so strict positivity is not guaranteed. Thus C.

Expand the square: E[(X-μ)²]=E[X²]-2μE[X]+μ²=E[X²]-μ², using μ=E[X]. Every squared deviation is nonnegative, and an expectation preserves nonnegativity. If X is always 5, R=25-25=0; if X is equally likely 0 or 1, R=1/2-1/4=1/4. These examples exclude both the claim that R is always zero and the claim that it is always strictly positive.

**Paraphrased problem context:** For positive integers x,y,z, let P(x)=NOT(x=1) AND ∀y((∃z(x=y*z)) ⇒ ((y=x) OR (y=1))). Which option correctly characterizes P(x)? The source says 'P(x) being true means' in A, B and D.

(A) x is prime
(B) x is a number other than 1
(C) P(x) is always true regardless of x
(D) x has exactly two factors other than 1 and x

## Solution

The predicate excludes1 and requires every positive divisor y of x to be either1 or x. Thus it is equivalent to x being prime, A. The wording P(x) being true means... is potentially only implication: under that reading B, x is a number other than1, is also true because it is explicitly a conjunct. A is the exact characterization and the official choice; uniqueness requires reading means as a full characterization rather than any necessary consequence.

Read the inner existential as 'y divides x': there exists a positive integer z with x=yz. The universal implication then says that whenever y is a divisor it is either 1 or x. For x=7 only these divisors occur, whereas x=6 fails at y=2,z=3. The leading conjunct rejects x=1. D incorrectly requires additional factors, and C fails for composites. B is a necessary consequence but not a sufficient description, as 6 is not 1 yet does not satisfy P.

**Paraphrased problem context:** For X=[x1,...,xn] with mean μx and standard deviation σx, define yi=a*xi+b with positive constants a,b. Y has mean μy and standard deviation σy. Which statement is INCORRECT?

(A) The index position of the mode in X corresponds to that in Y
(B) The index position of the median in X corresponds to that in Y
(C) μy=aμx+b
(D) σy=aσx+b

## Solution

For y_i=ax_i+b with a>0, ordering and frequencies are preserved, so modal and median positions correspond under consistent tie conventions. Means transform as μ_y=aμ_x+b. Deviations satisfy y_i−μ_y=a(x_i−μ_x), so standard deviation is σ_y=aσ_x, without+b. Thus D is incorrect; the same scaling holds for either population or sample normalization.

Translation by b shifts the center but leaves every distance from the center unchanged. Scaling by a multiplies each distance by a, each squared distance by a², and therefore the standard deviation by a after taking a square root. A simple check is a constant sample: its original standard deviation is zero and remains zero after any translation, while option D would incorrectly give b>0. Mode/median statements rely on the same consistent conventions when values tie.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Shuffle five cards numbered 1 through 5, and draw two sequentially without replacement. What is the probability that the first number is exactly one greater than the second?

(A) 1/5
(B) 4/25
(C) 1/4
(D) 2/5

## Solution

There are5×4=20 equally likely ordered pairs of distinct cards. The first exceeds the second by exactly1 for(2, 1),(3, 2),(4, 3),(5, 4), four cases. Probability4/20=1/5, A.

Ordered outcomes are the right sample space because the condition distinguishes the first and second cards. There are five choices initially and only four after the first removal. The favorable pairs exclude first card 1, because 0 is unavailable; each of first cards 2 through 5 permits exactly one second card. Using 25 as the denominator would incorrectly allow replacement, and counting both orders of each adjacent pair would answer the different absolute-difference question.
