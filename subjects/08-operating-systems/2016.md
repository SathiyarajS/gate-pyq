**Paraphrased problem context:** CPU-bound processes with unequal burst lengths all arrive simultaneously. Which scheduling policy minimizes average ready-queue waiting time? A: shortest remaining time first. B: round robin with quantum shorter than every burst. C: uniform random. D: highest priority first, with priority proportional to burst length.

## Solution

All processes are available initially and are CPU-bound, so shortest-remaining-time-first reduces to shortest-job-first. If a longer job immediately precedes a shorter one, swapping them reduces the sum of waiting times by the burst-length difference. Repeated exchanges yield increasing burst order, which is optimal. Thus A.

Suppose adjacent bursts have lengths a>b. Executing a before b contributes a to b's wait, whereas reversing them contributes b to a's wait; completion time of the pair and waits of later jobs stay unchanged. The swap decreases total wait by a−b. Eliminating all such inversions gives ascending burst order. Since all arrivals occur at time zero, no newly arriving short job triggers a distinct preemption issue; SRTF executes this same optimal order.

**Paraphrased problem context:** A system has 40-bit virtual addresses,16-kilobyte pages and a one-level page table per process. Each entry occupies 48 bits. Find the per-process page-table size in megabytes, using binary memory units.

## Solution

A16 KiB page uses 14 offset bits, leaving 40−14=26 virtual-page bits. There are 2^26 page-table entries, each 48 bits=6 bytes, totaling 6*2^26 bytes=384 MiB. The official answer uses binary kilobyte/megabyte conventions and excludes unspecified table metadata.

Page-table storage depends on the number of virtual pages, not the physical RAM capacity, which is not given. Offset bits are log 2(16×1024)=14; the remaining 26 bits index 2^26 entries. Convert the 48-bit entry into 6 bytes before multiplying. The product 402653184 bytes divided by 2^20 bytes per MiB is 384. Confusing 48 bits with 48 bytes would inflate the answer by a factor 8.

**Paraphrased problem context:** There are 10 initially empty physical page frames. The reference sequence is a1,a2,...,a 20,a1,a2,...,a 20, with 20 distinct pages. Find the difference between LIFO and optimal replacement in total page faults.

## Solution

Assume initially empty frames. Both policies fault on all 20 first-pass pages. LIFO retains a 1 through a 9 and the most recently inserted page a 20. The second pass hits on a 1..a 9 then faults on a 10..a 20, giving 31 faults total. OPT can retain a 1..a 9, a 20 after the first pass, then uses frames of already-consumed pages to service a 10..a 19 while retaining a 20, giving 30. At least 10 second-pass faults are unavoidable because only 10 of 20 distinct pages can initially be resident, so OPT is optimal. Difference 31−30=1.

Under LIFO, page hits do not change insertion order. Once frames fill with a1 through a 10, every first-pass fault replaces the newest page in the same slot; a1 through a9 remain throughout. In the second pass, these nine hit and the other eleven fault. OPT has ten resident pages at the start of the second pass and can fault on exactly the ten absent pages by discarding pages whose final reference has already occurred. The total faults are therefore 31 versus 30, not counts of distinct pages alone.

**Paraphrased problem context:** There are n processes Pi, and all t[i] initially equal 0. pmax returns an integer at least as large as each argument. Each Pi repeats:
c[i]=1; t[i]=pmax(t[0],...,t[n−1])+1; c[i]=0;
for every j!=i {while(c[j]); while(t[j]!=0 && t[j]<=t[i]);}
Critical Section; t[i]=0; Remainder Section;
Which claim is true? A:At most one process is in its critical section at any time. B:Bounded waiting holds. C:Progress holds. D:Deadlock cannot occur. Analyze the usual atomic-read/write, sequentially consistent, nonoverflowing-ticket model with initially clear choosing flags c.

## Solution

This is bakery-style ticket selection without a process-ID tie-breaker. The choosing flags ensure another process waits until ticket selection finishes. Under the usual atomic-read/write, sequentially consistent, nonoverflowing-ticket model, two active contenders cannot both pass each other: a smaller nonzero ticket blocks the larger, while equal tickets block both. Thus mutual exclusion holds. Two simultaneous selections can read the same old maximum and choose equal tickets; each then waits for the other forever, so progress, deadlock-freedom and eventual bounded waiting are not guaranteed. Only A. Choosing flags are understood initially clear.

For a concrete deadlock execution with two processes, both evaluate the old maximum 0 before either publishes its ticket, and both choose 1. After their choosing flags clear, each sees the other's nonzero equal ticket and waits because 1≤1. Neither reaches the code that resets its ticket to 0. A process-ID tie-breaker, as in the standard bakery algorithm, would order equal tickets; it is absent here. This shows why mutual exclusion can hold while progress and bounded waiting fail.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Which page-replacement policy can suffer a higher page-fault rate when more frames are allocated? A LRU; B OPT; C MRU; D FIFO.

## Solution

FIFO exhibits Belady’s anomaly. For reference string1, 2, 3, 4, 1, 2, 5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, FIFO produces9 faults with 3 frames but10 with 4. LRU and OPT obey inclusion/optimality monotonicity; MRU is also an inclusion policy under consistent recency/tie handling. Thus D.

With three FIFO frames the example’s fault positions are 1,2,3,4,5,6,7,10,11. With four they are 1,2,3,4,7,8,9,10,11,12. More capacity changes eviction order, not merely how many pages fit at one instant. The inclusion property prevents this anomaly when every smaller resident set is contained in the larger one.

**Derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** Two processes share turn=0. Process 0 entry waits while turn==1, then executes its critical section and sets turn=1 on exit. Process 1 waits while turn==0, executes its critical section and sets turn=0 on exit. A correct solution; B violates mutual exclusion; C violates progress; D violates bounded waiting.

## Solution

This is strict alternation. If process0 finishes its critical section and remains in its remainder section, turn becomes1. Process1 may enter and leave, setting turn back0. A further request by process1 is then blocked even though process0 is not competing. This violates progress. Mutual exclusion holds because turn cannot be both0 and 1; the intended answer is C under the standard synchronization-property definitions.

An even simpler progress witness occurs initially: only process 1 requests entry while process 0 stays forever in its remainder section. turn remains 0, so the empty critical section is inaccessible to the sole interested process. Strict alternation limits overtaking but lets an uninterested process prevent entry, which is why mutual exclusion alone is insufficient.

**Paraphrased problem context:** A nonnegative counting semaphore S uses P to decrement when permitted and V to increment. Twenty P and twelve V operations are issued in some order. Find the greatest initial S for which at least one P remains blocked after the V operations are processed.

## Solution

After all12 V operations have taken effect, the total number of permits available to satisfy the 20 P operations is S0+12. At least one remains blocked exactly when S0+12<20, giving the largest integer S0=7. This concerns operations remaining blocked after all issued V operations are processed, not a temporary block earlier in some ordering.

At S0=7 there are only 19 permits over the entire execution, so twenty P operations cannot all finish. At S0=8 there are twenty permits, enough eventually to release every issued P once all twelve V operations occur. A transient empty semaphore does not prove an operation will remain blocked after future signals.
