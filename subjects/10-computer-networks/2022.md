**Official answer:** C

**Derived answer:** C

**Paraphrased problem context:** Count internal enterprise subnets in this topology: Internet—firewall; firewall—left router; firewall—top router; top router—right router; top router—web server; left router—Ethernet LAN 1; right router—Ethernet LAN 2. Each LAN has several hosts. Exclude the Internet-facing external link. A 3; B 12; C 6; D 8.

## Solution

Count the separately routed internal links/LANs: two Ethernet LANs, firewall–left router, firewall–top router, top router–right router, and top router–web server. These are six subnets. The external Internet-facing link is outside the enterprise count. Thus C under the depicted routed-interface model. All connections were visually checked.

Teaching detail:
A subnet groups interfaces sharing a layer-3 link, not individual computers. Therefore each multi-host Ethernet contributes one, not its host count. The four remaining internal point-to-point/server links each contribute one, making 2+4=6.

**Official answer:** 4 to 4

**Derived answer:** 4 under one delegation per named level

**Paraphrased problem context:** An uncached DNS resolver iteratively resolves www.gate.org.in. Under the intended separate-delegation model for each domain level, how many query-response pairs are required? Distinguish this convention from what the domain name alone guarantees.

## Solution

In the intended uncached iterative resolution, query the root, then the in server, then org.in, then gate.org.in for the www record. That is four query-response pairs. This counts the resolver’s outbound iterative exchanges, not a separate stub-to-resolver request. Four assumes a separate delegation at every shown domain level and no additional aliases; domain labels alone do not guarantee actual zone boundaries.

Teaching detail:
The logical exchange sequence is resolver→root (referral to .in), →.in (referral to org.in), →org.in (referral to gate.org.in), →gate.org.in (www address). Each request together with its response counts as one pair, not two. Real deployments can combine labels into one zone, so this is the examination hierarchy assumption.

**Official answer:** B,D

**Derived answer:** B,D

**Paraphrased problem context:** Routing entries are 12.20.164.0 mask 255.255.252.0→R1; 12.20.170.0 mask 255.255.254.0→R2; 12.20.168.0 with /23→interface 0; 12.20.166.0 with /23→interface 1; default→R3. Which offered prefixes collectively aggregate the nondefault address set? A 12.20.164.0/20; B 12.20.164.0/22; C 12.20.164.0/21; D 12.20.168.0/22.

## Solution

The nondefault entries cover third-octet ranges 164-167, 170-171, 168-169 and 166-167. Their union is exactly 164-171. It is covered by the two aligned prefixes 12.20.164.0/22 (164-167) and 12.20.168.0/22 (168-171), giving B, D. A /21 containing 164 begins at 160 and covers 160-167; a /20 containing it begins at 160 and covers 160-175. The offered /21 and /20 addresses are not canonical network starts and would include extra destinations when masked. This answer describes address-set aggregation, not replacing all forwarding entries with two routes while preserving their different next hops; the more-specific routes are still needed for those next-hop distinctions.

Teaching detail:
A /22 fixes the top six bits of the third octet, so its network begins on a multiple of four and covers four consecutive values. Ranges 164–167 and 168–171 straddle a /21 boundary and cannot be replaced by the offered /21 without changing the address set. Longest-prefix forwarding details remain distinct from this coverage calculation.

**Type / marks:** NAT, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** 0.5 to 0.5

**Derived answer:** 0.5

**Paraphrased problem context:** Unit-cost routers form P—Q—R. Distance-vector tables have converged when Q—R fails. P and Q send updates at random times at the same average rate. Find, to one decimal place, the intended probability of a P↔Q loop and count-to-infinity; explain the stochastic assumptions needed.

## Solution

Before failure, P believes R is two hops away via Q, and Q has a one-hop route. After detecting Q-R failure, Q can be misled if P advertises its stale route first: Q installs distance 3 via P while P still routes via Q, forming a loop. If Q announces unreachability first, P removes the route and avoids that loop. In the intended symmetric independent update-race model, either router is equally likely to send first, so the probability is 1/2=0.5. The stated equality of average update rates alone does not mathematically specify this race probability: identical/exchangeable timing distributions and no relevant delivery delay are additional assumptions. The example also assumes ordinary distance vector without split horizon or poisoned reverse; those safeguards change the result.

Teaching detail:
For independent exponential update clocks of rate λ, P(P sends first)=∫₀∞ λe^(−λt)e^(−λt)dt=1/2. Equal mean intervals without identical residual-time distributions do not justify this integral. In the loop, Q installs 3 via P, then P learns 4 via Q, then Q learns 5, illustrating count-to-infinity.

**Official answer:** 7.07 to 7.09

**Derived answer:** 7.08

**Paraphrased problem context:** A 100 Mbps earth-to-satellite link spans 2100 km; propagation speed is 3×10^8 m/s. How many milliseconds elapse from starting transmission until a 1000-byte packet is completely received? Round to two decimals.

## Solution

Propagation takes 2100000/(3*10^8)=0.007 seconds=7 ms. Serializing 1000 bytes at 100*10^6 bits per second takes 8000/(100*10^6)=0.00008 seconds=0.08 ms. Complete reception occurs after one propagation delay plus serialization, not a round trip. Total 7.08 ms lies in the official 7.07-7.09 range.

Teaching detail:
Use decimal networking units: 100 Mbps=100,000,000 bits/s and 1000 bytes=8000 bits. The first bit arrives at 7 ms; the last bit starts 0.08 ms later and experiences the same propagation delay, arriving at 7.08 ms. Acknowledgment or round-trip delays are not requested.

**Official answer:** 33 to 33

**Derived answer:** 33

**Paraphrased problem context:** TCP sends over 1 Gbps with maximum segment lifetime 60 seconds. What minimum sequence-number bit width avoids wrapping during that lifetime?

## Solution

TCP sequence numbers identify bytes. At 10^9 bits per second, the stream uses 10^9/8=125000000 sequence numbers per second and 7500000000 in 60 seconds. A nonwrapping sequence space must accommodate that many distinct bytes during the MSL. Since 2^32=4294967296 is too small but 2^33=8589934592 is enough, 33 bits are required.

Teaching detail:
The bound is 2^b≥rate_in_bytes_per_second×MSL. Taking a ceiling gives b=ceil(log2(7.5×10^9))=33. Counting bits rather than bytes would give the wrong sequence-space requirement because TCP increments sequence numbers per payload byte.
