**Paraphrased problem context:** For IPv4 routing, assess I:RIP uses distance-vector routing; II:RIP packets use UDP; III:OSPF packets use TCP; IV:OSPF uses link-state routing. A:I,IV. B:I,II,III. C:I,II,IV. D:II,III,IV.

## Solution

1. RIP exchanges distance estimates with neighbors and uses a distance-vector approach, so I is true. Its routing messages are carried in UDP datagrams, making II true.

2. OSPF distributes link-state information and computes shortest paths from its link-state database, so IV is true. Its packets run directly over IP using protocol number 89, not through TCP, so III is false.

3. The true set is I,II,IV, option C. Routing algorithm type and transport encapsulation are separate properties; identifying OSPF as link-state does not imply that it uses TCP.

# GATE 2017 CS2: Q28 (printed CS Q18)

**Question:** [Official archived paper page 7](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1popAHO-cX69oQEx_tD0wZXL1tTrBljeO/view); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1 mark(s).

**Official answer:** B

**Derived answer:** B under single bidirectional peer interpretation

**Source access:** Open archive member CS/CS2-2017.pdf inside the linked official CS.zip. Page numbers refer to the extracted PDF, not the Drive preview.

**Paraphrased problem context:** On Linux, a connected UDP socket is one on which connect has been called. Assess I:A connected UDP socket can communicate with multiple peers simultaneously. II:A process can successfully call connect again on an already connected UDP socket. A:I only. B:II only. C:both. D:neither.

## Solution

1. UDP connect does not establish a TCP-like reliable connection. It associates a default destination with the socket and ordinarily restricts incoming datagrams to that peer, allowing send/write calls without specifying an address.

2. Another connect call can change the peer association, so II is true. Under the exam's interpretation of communicate as simultaneous default, bidirectional peer associations, I is false and the intended answer is B.

3. Preserve the Linux API caveat: a connected UDP socket may still send a datagram to another destination explicitly supplied to sendto/sendmsg. Thus it is inaccurate to say that a connected UDP socket can never send to multiple peers. The unique B answer relies on the bidirectional/default-peer interpretation. The alternate-destination behavior is documented at https://www.man7.org/linux/man-pages/man7/udp.7.html.

**Paraphrased problem context:** What is the maximum number of IPv4 router addresses that can be listed in the Record Route (RR) option field of one IPv4 header?

## Solution

1. The IPv4 IHL field can specify at most 15 words of 4 bytes, giving a 60-byte maximum header. The fixed header takes 20 bytes, leaving at most 40 for options and padding.

2. The Record Route option uses three control bytes: type, length and pointer. Therefore at most 40-3=37 bytes remain for recorded addresses. Each IPv4 address needs 4 bytes.

3. The maximum integer count is floor(37/4)=9. Nine addresses occupy 36 bytes plus 3 control bytes, and one padding byte brings the header options area to 40. Ten addresses would need 43 option bytes and cannot fit.

**Paraphrased problem context:** A binary code has valid words 00000,01011,10101,11110. Let p be its minimum Hamming distance and q the maximum guaranteed correctable number of bit errors. A:p=3,q=1. B:p=3,q=2. C:p=4,q=1. D:p=4,q=2.

## Solution

1. Hamming distance counts differing positions, obtainable by counting ones in the XOR. Against 00000 the other three words have weights 3,3,4, giving the first three distances.

2. The remaining pairs have distances d(01011,10101)=4, d(01011,11110)=3 and d(10101,11110)=3. Hence the minimum over all six pairs is p=3.

3. To correct every pattern of q errors, radius-q neighborhoods of distinct codewords must not overlap, requiring 2q<p. Thus q=floor((p-1)/2)=1. Select A. A distance-3 code can detect two errors, but guaranteed correction of two errors would require minimum distance at least 5.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Hosts X and Y have a direct link of rate 10^6 bits/s, length 10,000 km, and propagation speed 2×10^8 m/s. X continuously sends 50,000 bytes as one large message. Find transmission p and propagation q delays in ms. A:(50,100). B:(50,400). C:(100,50). D:(400,50).

## Solution

1. Transmission delay is the time to put all message bits onto the link. Convert bytes to bits: 50,000×8=400,000 bits. Divide by 10^6 bits/s to get 0.4 seconds, or p=400 ms.

2. Propagation delay is the travel time of a bit from one end to the other. Convert the distance: 10,000 km=10^7 m. Divide by 2×10^8 m/s to get 0.05 seconds, or q=50 ms.

3. Thus D gives the requested ordered pair. These are different delays: message size affects transmission, while distance affects propagation. Under the ideal assumptions the last bit arrives after their sum, 450 ms, but the question asks for the two components separately.

**Paraphrased problem context:** After TCP data transfer, a client calls close, sends FIN, and receives the server’s ACK. In which RFC793 state does the client now wait for the server FIN? A LAST-ACK; B TIME-WAIT; C FIN-WAIT-1; D FIN-WAIT-2.

## Solution

1. The client is the active closer because it sends FIN first. Sending FIN moves it from ESTABLISHED into FIN-WAIT-1.

2. The ACK acknowledges that outgoing FIN. The client has finished its own send direction but the server has not yet sent its FIN, so it enters FIN-WAIT-2.

3. Receiving the peer FIN and acknowledging it leads to TIME-WAIT. LAST-ACK belongs to the passive closer awaiting acknowledgment of its own FIN. Therefore D.

**Paraphrased problem context:** S digitally signs message m with its private key and sends it to R. Who can use a birthday attack to replace m with a fraudulent message? I S; II a third-party attacker; III R. A I and II; B I only; C II only; D II and III.

## Solution

1. A birthday collision attack searches for any two distinct messages with the same digest; it does not efficiently target one already fixed digest. For an n-bit digest its generic collision cost is about 2^(n/2), versus 2^n for a fixed-target second preimage.

2. The signer can prepare many harmless and fraudulent variants before selecting what to sign, find a colliding pair, and reuse the signature on the fraudulent member. This makes I possible in the intended hash-then-sign setting.

3. The receiver or outside attacker only receives the fixed signed m. Replacing that requires a second preimage, not the freely chosen birthday pair. Thus II and III do not follow, and B is intended.

1. The degree-three generator requires three check bits. Append three zeros to 01011011, obtaining dividend 01011011000; generator bits are 1011.

3. Replace the three zero placeholders with 101: transmitted bits are 01011011101, C. Dividing this final codeword by 1011 yields remainder zero, an independent check.

**Paraphrased problem context:** RSA uses primes p=13,q=17 and public exponent 35. Find the private exponent (least positive modular inverse).

## Solution

1. RSA modulus N=pq=13×17=221. Since p and q are distinct primes, φ(N)=(p−1)(q−1)=12×16=192.

2. The private exponent d must satisfy ed≡1 mod φ(N), so solve 35d≡1 mod192. Euclid gives 192=5×35+17 and 35=2×17+1.

3. Back substitute: 1=35−2(192−5×35)=11×35−2×192. Hence d=11 is the least positive inverse. Check 35×11=385=2×192+1.

No errors. Find useful-data transmission efficiency as a percentage to two decimals.

## Solution

1. Interpret 1980 bytes as total information-frame length including its 20 overhead bytes. Useful payload=1960 bytes, so useful transmission time is 1960×8/10^6 s=15.68 ms.

3. Efficiency=useful payload time/cycle time=15.68/18=0.871111..., or 87.11%. If “1980” instead denotes payload before overhead, use 15.84/18.16, giving 87.22%. Both lie in the preserved archived-key interval 86.5–89.5; do not silently replace that interval with one exact value.
