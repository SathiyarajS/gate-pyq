**Paraphrased problem context:** Choose the antonym of protagonist from: A agnostic, B antagonist, C arsonist, D anarchist.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1.

**Official answer:** B

**Derived answer:** B

## Solution

Givens and goal
Choose the antonym of protagonist from: A agnostic, B antagonist, C arsonist, D anarchist.

Approach
Compare the meanings in the context of characters in a narrative, rather than choosing a word with a similar sound.

Step-by-step solution
1. A protagonist is the main character around whom a story is organized. The word describes a role, not necessarily a morally good person.
2. An antagonist is the character or force opposing that protagonist. It is the contrasting narrative role and therefore the intended antonym.
3. An agnostic does not claim knowledge about the existence of a deity; an arsonist deliberately sets fires; an anarchist advocates or favors the absence of imposed government. None names the opposing role in a story.
4. Therefore select B, antagonist. A villain can be the protagonist of a story, so equating protagonist with “good person” would be an unreliable way to solve the question.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1.

**Official answer:** C

**Derived answer:** C

## Solution

Givens and goal
The permitted tiles are a 2-by-2 square and a 1-by-4 bar, each made of four unit squares. Either may be reused and rotated, with no overlaps. The candidate target rectangles are A: 2 by 4, B: 3 by 4, C: 3 by 5, D: 4 by 5. Identify the one that cannot be tiled.

Approach
First use area as a necessary condition, then construct explicit tilings for the remaining options.

Step-by-step solution
1. If a target uses r square tiles and s bar tiles, its area is 4r+4s=4(r+s). Therefore its number of unit cells must be divisible by 4. Rotation does not change this invariant.
2. The four target areas are 8, 12, 15 and 20. Since 15 is not divisible by 4, option C cannot be formed from whole permitted tiles without overlaps or gaps.
3. Divisibility alone is not sufficient for every tiling problem, so check the other shapes constructively. A can be covered by two horizontal 1-by-4 bars, one per row. B can be covered by three such bars. D can be covered by five vertical 4-by-1 bars, one per column.
4. These constructions prove that A, B and D are possible, leaving only C impossible. The 2-by-2 piece need not be used because the wording permits either piece alone.

**Paraphrased problem context:** A knockout singles tournament begins with 64 players. Every game has one loser, who leaves the tournament; play ends with one unbeaten winner. Find the total number of games. Choices: 127, 64, 63, 32.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1.

**Official answer:** C

**Derived answer:** C

## Solution

Givens and goal
A knockout singles tournament begins with 64 players. Every game has one loser, who leaves the tournament; play ends with one unbeaten winner. Find the total number of games. Choices: 127, 64, 63, 32.

Approach
Count how many eliminations are required instead of trying to enumerate all possible bracket arrangements.

Step-by-step solution
1. Exactly one of the 64 entrants remains at the end, so 64−1=63 entrants must be eliminated.
2. A game eliminates exactly one entrant because there are no ties and the loser leaves immediately. It cannot eliminate two, and the eventual winner is never eliminated.
3. Thus 63 eliminations require exactly 63 games, giving option C.
4. As a check, a balanced bracket has 32 first-round games, then 16, 8, 4, 2 and 1. Their sum is 63. The elimination argument remains valid even if the schedule is not balanced; 32 counts only the first round, while 64 incorrectly counts a loss for the champion.

**Paraphrased problem context:** A student must take between 60 and 70 credits in total, including exactly 15 core credits, exactly 20 project credits, and at least 10 specialization credits. Find the maximum elective credits; the choices are 10, 15, 20 and 25.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1.

**Official answer:** D

**Derived answer:** D

## Solution

Givens and goal
A student must take between 60 and 70 credits in total, including exactly 15 core credits, exactly 20 project credits, and at least 10 specialization credits. Find the maximum elective credits; the choices are 10, 15, 20 and 25.

Approach
Write the total-credit inequality and minimize the compulsory non-elective allocation.

Step-by-step solution
1. Let s be specialization credits and e elective credits. The total is 15+20+s+e=35+s+e, with s≥10 and 60≤35+s+e≤70.
2. The upper constraint gives e≤35−s. Since s≥10, it follows that e≤25. Increasing specialization above its minimum only reduces the capacity available for electives.
3. To show the bound is attainable, choose s=10 and e=25. The total becomes 15+20+10+25=70, which satisfies both the minimum 60 and maximum 70.
4. Therefore the maximum is 25 credits, option D. Using the minimum total 60 would give 15 elective credits at s=10, but the question asks for the largest allowed elective allocation, not the minimum needed to graduate.

**Paraphrased problem context:** The premise says that whenever the teacher is in the room, every student stands silently. Which claim need not follow? A: a student not standing silently implies the teacher is absent; B: teacher present implies all silent; C: all standing implies teacher present; D: teacher present implies all standing.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 1.

**Official answer:** C

**Derived answer:** C

## Solution

Givens and goal
The premise says that whenever the teacher is in the room, every student stands silently. Which claim need not follow? A: a student not standing silently implies the teacher is absent; B: teacher present implies all silent; C: all standing implies teacher present; D: teacher present implies all standing.

Approach
Translate the premise into an implication, separate its consequences, and distinguish the contrapositive from the converse.

Step-by-step solution
1. Let T mean “teacher present”, S mean “all students standing”, and Q mean “all students silent”. The premise is T⇒(S∧Q).
2. If T is true, both conjuncts must be true. This proves B, T⇒Q, and D, T⇒S.
3. The contrapositive is ¬(S∧Q)⇒¬T. If any student is not standing silently, the conjunction fails, so the teacher cannot be present. That is A.
4. The converse S⇒T is not implied. A valid counterexample has the teacher absent but all students standing, perhaps while waiting for the teacher. The premise remains true because it imposes a condition only when the teacher is present.
5. Hence C is the statement that is not necessarily true. The counterexample need not prove C always false; one permitted situation in which C fails is enough.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2.

**Official answer:** B

**Derived answer:** B

## Solution

Givens and goal
A passage describes combinatorial counting and says that it is sometimes used to model physical phenomena; probabilities are often assigned to the possibilities to calculate average quantities. P claims combinatorics is always used in physical modeling. Q claims some physical models assign probabilities to combinatorial possibilities to obtain averages. Classify P and Q.

Approach
Track the quantifiers “sometimes/some” and “always”, without broadening what the passage actually supports.

Step-by-step solution
1. The passage gives a particular use of combinatorics: count possible arrangements or outcomes, assign probabilities, and average a physical quantity over those outcomes.
2. This is precisely the existential, limited claim in Q. It says some models work this way, not that every model must, so Q is supported.
3. P replaces that limited claim with “always”. The passage supplies no basis for saying that every model of every physical phenomenon invokes combinatorics. The intended reading-comprehension classification rejects P and accepts Q.
4. Therefore the published answer is B: P false and Q true. A rejects the supported statement Q; C and D accept the unsupported universal assertion P.

Logical qualification: “not established by the passage” is not, in formal logic, the same as “its negation has been proved”. The passage's word “sometimes” does not itself exhibit a physical model that never uses combinatorics. Preserve the official passage-based interpretation rather than presenting this as a strict mathematical proof of ¬P.

## Solution

Givens and goal
For S,K>0, define H_K(S)=max(S−K,0). The plotted N(S) is zero through S=10, rises linearly from (10,0) to (20,10), and stays at 10 afterwards. Choices: A H_10−H_20; B H_10−2H_20; C −H_10+H_20; D H_15−H_20.

Approach
Transcribe the graph as a piecewise function, then activate the hinge terms one interval at a time.

Step-by-step solution
1. The middle line has slope (10−0)/(20−10)=1. Hence N(S)=0 for 0<S≤10, N(S)=S−10 for 10<S≤20, and N(S)=10 for S>20.
2. H_10 starts a slope-1 ramp at 10. On its own it would continue increasing beyond 20, so subtract a second slope-1 ramp that starts at 20.
3. Evaluate A=H_10−H_20. Below 10 both terms vanish. Between 10 and 20 only H_10 is active, giving S−10. Above 20, the result is (S−10)−(S−20)=10. It also takes the correct endpoint values at 10 and 20.
4. B instead has slope 1−2=−1 above 20 and falls after the peak. C is the negative of A. D starts rising too late, at 15, and reaches a plateau of only 5. Thus only A matches every segment of the graph.

**Paraphrased problem context:** In a six-round javelin final, Chopra wins gold with his second-round throw, Vadlejch wins silver and Vesely bronze. Neither silver nor bronze athlete makes a medal-winning throw in rounds 1–2. Their sixth throws are fouls. Vesely is second after four rounds and never improves later. Vadlejch has the best throw of round 4. Determine the round of Vesely’s best throw: 3, 4, 5 or 6.

**Type / marks:** MCQ, 2.

**Official answer:** A

**Derived answer:** A

## Solution

Givens and goal
In a six-round javelin final, Chopra wins gold with his second-round throw, Vadlejch wins silver and Vesely bronze. Neither silver nor bronze athlete makes a medal-winning throw in rounds 1–2. Their sixth throws are fouls. Vesely is second after four rounds and never improves later. Vadlejch has the best throw of round 4. Determine the round of Vesely’s best throw: 3, 4, 5 or 6.

Approach
Eliminate rounds inconsistent with the observations, using the ranking after round 4 to distinguish rounds 3 and 4.

Step-by-step solution
1. Vesely’s eventual best is his bronze-medal throw. The premise excludes medal-winning throws by him in rounds 1 and 2, so neither can be his best.
2. He has reached his best by the end of round 4 and does not improve in rounds 5 or 6. In particular, round 6 is a foul, so the winning distance must already occur in round 3 or 4.
3. Suppose his best occurred in round 4. Vadlejch’s throw is the best in that round, so it is farther than Vesely’s round-4 throw and hence farther than Vesely’s best through round 4.
4. Chopra is already ahead with his gold-winning round-2 throw. Vadlejch would therefore also be ahead of Vesely after round 4, putting Vesely no higher than third. This contradicts the stated second position.
5. Round 4 is impossible; the remaining round is 3, option A. This argument uses the stated strict ranking of the throws, rather than the athletes’ real-world distances.

**Official answer:** C

**Derived answer:** C

## Solution

Givens and goal
A fair six-sided die labeled 1–6 is rolled twice. Find the probability that the second result is an integer multiple of the first. Options: 1/6, 5/18, 7/18, 5/6.

Approach
Count favorable ordered pairs by fixing the first result; multiplication by 1 is allowed.

Step-by-step solution
1. The outcomes are ordered pairs (first,second). Independence and fairness give 6×6=36 equally likely outcomes.
2. If the first result is 1, every second result qualifies: 1,2,3,4,5,6. If it is 2, the qualifying second results are 2,4,6. For 3 they are 3,6. For 4,5,6 they are respectively only 4, only 5, and only 6.
3. The favorable counts are therefore 6+3+2+1+1+1=14. Equivalently, sum floor(6/a) over a=1,…,6.
4. The probability is 14/36=7/18, so select C. Equal results must count because a=1·a. Conversely, (2,4) qualifies but (4,2) does not; treating the rolls as unordered pairs would use the wrong sample space.

## Solution

Givens and goal
A square has been cut into two pieces. Identify its missing piece among A (i), B (ii), C (iii), D (iv). Approach
Treat the missing piece as the geometric complement of the retained piece, not as a merely similar silhouette.

Step-by-step solution
1. Overlay a unit grid following the equally spaced horizontal and vertical edges. The top and bottom full boundary strips and leftmost full column belong to the retained part. Complement the remaining cells and discard those empty outside rows and column to obtain a four-row, five-column missing-piece mask.
