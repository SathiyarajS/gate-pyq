**Paraphrased problem context:** Choose the grammatically and idiomatically correct sentence. A: I will not leave the place until the minister does not meet me. B: I will not leave the place until the minister doesn't meet me. C: I will not leave the place until the minister meet me. D: I will not leave the place until the minister meets me.

## Solution

Until introduces the positive event that ends the refusal: I will not leave until the minister meets me. Singular minister requires meets, and a second negation reverses the intended condition. Thus D.

The word until marks the event that ends the waiting period. The intended meaning is that leaving becomes possible after a meeting occurs, not after a refusal to meet. In a future time clause, English normally uses present tense: “until he meets,” even though the main clause says “will.” A and B have the same unnecessary negation; C lacks the third-person singular ending. These are separate meaning and agreement checks, both satisfied by D.

**Paraphrased problem context:** A rewording of something written or spoken is a _____. A: paraphrase. B: paradox. C: paradigm. D: paraffin.

## Solution

A paraphrase restates written or spoken material in different words while preserving its meaning. Thus A.

The defining feature is preservation of meaning while changing wording. For example, “She departed early” paraphrases “She left ahead of time.” A paradox is an apparently contradictory statement, a paradigm is a model or pattern, and paraffin is a substance. Matching the definition to the word is more reliable than choosing solely by the shared prefix para-.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Archimedes said, “Give me a lever long enough and a fulcrum on which to place it, and I will move the world.” What kind of statement is this? A: figurative. B: collateral. C: literal. D: figurine.

## Solution

Moving the world with a lever is an exaggerated illustration of leverage, not a literal proposal. This is figurative language, A.

The image dramatizes the mechanical advantage of a lever by using an impossible scale. Figurative language includes this kind of hyperbole. Literal would mean the statement describes an actual practical undertaking without exaggeration; figurine means a small statue and is not the adjective for figurative speech. Collateral means accompanying or secondary and does not classify this rhetorical use.

**Paraphrased problem context:** In a code, relftaga means carefree, otaga means careful, and fertaga means careless. Which could mean aftercare? A: zentaga. B: tagafer. C: tagazen. D: relffer.

## Solution

The shared element taga corresponds to care. In all three examples the other English component follows care but its coded component precedes taga, so the component order is reversed. Aftercare therefore puts taga first, followed by a new component for after: tagazen, C.

Compare repeated parts rather than guessing whole words. Care is common to all three meanings, and taga is common to all three codes. **Paraphrased problem context:** A large cube is assembled from 64 unit-side cubes. One unit cube is removed from each of its eight corners. Find the surface area of the remaining solid in square units. A: 56. B: 64. C: 72. D: 96.

## Solution

The original cube has side 4 and surface area 6·4²=96. Removing a corner unit cube removes three exposed unit faces but reveals three previously internal unit faces. The eight corner removals do not overlap, so net area change is zero. Thus 96, D.

A useful distinction is between surface area and volume. The volume falls from 64 to 56, but 56 is not the surface area. At each removed corner, the lost faces are the three exterior faces meeting at that corner; its other three faces were touching neighboring cubes and become newly exposed. Counting all newly exposed faces, including the inward-facing recess surfaces, leaves the total unchanged. Since the side length is 4, different corner cuts do not remove adjacent cubes or overlap one another's exposed faces.

**Paraphrased problem context:** Four poker players satisfy: P always beats Q; R always beats S; S loses to P only sometimes; R always loses to Q. Which conclusions follow? (i) P is likely to beat all three others. (ii) S is the absolute worst player. A: i only. B: ii only. C: both. D: neither.

## Solution

The premises give pairwise outcomes, not a transitive total skill ranking or numerical winning probabilities. P sometimes loses to S, and no P-versus-R outcome is supplied, so i is not entailed. S sometimes beats P, which rules out the asserted absolute-worst conclusion ii. Therefore neither follows, D.

Do not silently make “beats” a transitive relation: a beats b and b beats c do not logically entail a beats c in a game. Nor does “sometimes” provide a numerical win probability. Thus the given individual matchup statements cannot establish that P is likely to defeat everyone. S has victories against P, so an absolute bottom ranking is not established either. The test is what the premises entail, not a plausible informal ranking from selected outcomes.
