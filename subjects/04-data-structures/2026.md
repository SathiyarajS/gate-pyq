**Question summary (paraphrased):** Find the greatest edge-height achievable by a full binary tree containing 23 nodes.

Accepted numerical interval is preserved exactly.

**Type / marks:** NAT, 1 mark. Paper variant CS1 (forenoon); the official key labels the exam session 3.

**Official answer:** 11 to 11

**Derived answer:** 11

## Solution

A full binary tree has either zero or two children at each node. If it has I internal nodes and L leaves, then 2I = I+L-1, so L=I+1 and the total is 2I+1. For 23 nodes, I=11. A root-to-leaf path of h edges contains h internal nodes, so h cannot exceed 11. This bound is achieved by a comb-shaped full tree: every internal node except the last has one leaf child and one internal child, while the last has two leaf children. Its height is 11 edges.

# GATE 2026 CS1: Q23

**Status:** Partial coverage. Independently derived and published official-key matched; independent second derivation review passed; final-key revision check pending.

**Question summary (paraphrased):** In a one-indexed binary heap with odd n>100, identify nonleaf positions among (n+1)/2, (n-1)/2, (n-3)/2 and n.

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 1 mark.

**Official answer:** B;C

## Solution

At index i in a one-indexed binary heap, a left child exists exactly when 2i <= n. Hence internal nodes have indices 1 through floor(n/2), and leaves have indices floor(n/2)+1 through n.

Write n=2k+1. The four listed positions become k+1, k, k-1 and 2k+1. The internal-node boundary is k.

Positions k and k-1 are internal, while k+1 and 2k+1 are leaves. The condition n>100 ensures k-1 is a valid positive index. Therefore choose B and C.

**Common pitfall:** Heap ordering does not determine leaf positions; the complete-binary-tree shape and indexing do.

## Q40 — Data Structures: insertion constraints in a complete BST

Paper page 29; key page 2. MCQ, 2 marks. **Answer: D, value 5.**

1. A complete binary tree with 15 nodes has all four levels full. For the ordered keys 1 through 15, the BST invariant therefore fixes root 8, its children 4 and 12, and their children 2, 6, 10 and 14. The leaves are 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13 and 15.
2. Ordinary BST insertion without rotations requires every ancestor of a node to have been inserted earlier. Otherwise an earlier inserted descendant would occupy an ancestor position and could not later move below it.
3. The ancestors of 5 are 8, 4 and 6. All three must already exist before 5 is inserted, so 5 cannot be inserted third.
4. Each other offered value can occur third: prefixes (8,12,4), (8,4,2) and (8,12,10) place 4, 2 and 10 third respectively. Each prefix respects the fixed tree's ancestor order, so it can be extended by inserting the remaining keys in an ancestor-before-descendant order to produce the required complete tree.

**Common error:** A completed tree need not stay complete at every intermediate insertion. Only the final shape is constrained here.

# GATE 2026 CS2: Q49

**Question:** [Official master paper page 35](https://gate2026.iitg.ac.in/doc/download/2026/QPs/CS2.pdf#page=35); consult original wording, diagrams and choices.

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** A;C

**Derived answer:** A;C

## Solution

The open intervals between a leaf key and its inorder successor are disjoint for different leaves, because successors are consecutive in sorted order. New keys chosen in them therefore cannot coincide, proving A. Each selected key follows the old leaf’s search path and becomes its right child: there is no existing key between that leaf and its successor, and the leaf has no children. Insertions associated with different leaf intervals do not alter one another’s routes, so every new node is at most one level below an old leaf; height increases by at most one, proving C. B is false for a singleton tree, or a right-only chain: its only leaf is the maximum and has no successor, so K is empty. These examples also refute D. Select A,C.

# GATE 2026 CS2: Q50

**Type / marks:** MSQ, 2 mark(s).

**Official answer:** A;B

**Derived answer:** A;B

## Solution

The stack emits its assigned subsequence in reverse arrival order, followed by the queue’s assigned subsequence in arrival order. A assigns stack {1,3,4}, yielding 4,3,1, then queue {2,5}, yielding 2,5. B assigns stack {3,4}, yielding 4,3, then queue {1,2,5}, yielding 1,2,5. Both produce the requested output. C produces 5,4,1,2,3 and D produces 3,2,1,4,5, so neither works. Thus A,B. The boundary between the stack prefix and queue suffix is not uniquely determined by this output.
